INTRODUCTION AND PROJECT SUMMARY

This is a Monthly Scientific and Technical Report for an ONR project entitled Advanced Perception for
Autonomous Platforms in the Littorals ". The objectives of the effort in this period were (1) calibration,
rectification, and field deployment of the M364C FLIR camera system to enhance IR-RGB alignment and
expand the IMSEL Maritime Dataset to 8,589 annotated image-label pairs; (2) develop a fusion-based
approach to reliably detect small, low-RCS objects at sea by combining radar and camera data, and use
radar cues to streamline image labeling.; (3) focused on accuracy testing and algorithmic imprvements for
the orthorectification process from the camera system used for surface current measurements; (4) fusing
radar camera and INS data on the Jetson Orin; (5) rain segmentation and data cleaning for the deraining
task.; (6) optimal camera calibration for different geometric range distance estimates; (7) increase
robustness of long wave IR range estimates; (8) assess deep learning as a method for range estimates; (9)
assess uncertainty in the geometric range estimates; and (10) create a virtual ocean environment with
shorelines approaches for model training and real time shoreline characterization.

replacing fixed-point image cropping with a world coordinate distance cutoff method. This approach
prevents errors related to projecting distant pixels and eliminates the need for manual cutoff tuning.
The system now identifies edge pixels that exceed a specified world distance and uses the resulting
points to define the bounds of the rectified output. This improvement increases both the reliability and
scalability of the orthorectification process.

the fusion of radar, camera, and INS sensors into a unified data collection platform. Timestamped
radar sweeps are now stored alongside synchronized camera images and INS metadata in a structured
format suitable for downstream processing. Real-time validation confirmed accurate operation across
all streams, with no data loss or misalignment. The resulting dataset enables precise time-correlated
analysis for sensor fusion applications.

2.2.2

Sea-ing is Believing: Automated Marine Object Labeling

Part 1 Introduction & Background
In maritime settings, vision-based object detection faces a severe data labeling bottleneck. Building large,
annotated image datasets of small boats or floating objects at sea is expensive and time-consuming. Such
objects, as seen in Figure 17, are relatively rare and often only a few pixels in size, so collecting and labeling
examples requires expert annotators to sift through hours of mostly-empty footage.

Page 13 of 55
DISTRIBUTION STATEMENT D

Figure 17: Small Object Detection: A Succinct Review
Existing public maritime image datasets are few, and those that do exist have limited scale and diversity.
Deep learning models demand plentiful labeled examples to achieve high accuracy, yet acquiring those
examples on the open ocean is impractical. As a result, current vision models trained on sparse maritime
data struggle to detect small, distant objects reliably. In comparison to common object detection on land,
labeling maritime scenes (mostly water with tiny far-off targets) is far more costly and difficult. This
shortage of annotated data is a major barrier to deploying robust camera-based detectors on marine
platforms.
Secondary sensor suites, such as marine radar systems, often have difficulty reliably detecting very small,
low-RCS targets (e.g. a partially submerged person, small buoy, or debris) against the dynamic ocean
background. Such objects return very weak echoes that are easily masked by sea clutter, the radar returns
from waves. In practice, the unfavorable signal-to-clutter ratio means that small craft or persons in the water
may go undetected by conventional X-band navigation radars in rough seas. Even specialized surveillance
radars (for example, airborne periscope-detection radars) suffer high false-alarm rates in choppy conditions,
as breaking wave tops produce spurious bright clutter spikes that can mimic or eclipse genuine targets. In
short, using radar alone, small maritime objects are often lost in clutter or generate many false detections.
An example of Low-RCS objects in differing clutter environments is provided in Figure 18.

This represents a critical gap for maritime safety and security, as low-profile hazards or intruders may elude
single-sensor detection. However, Radar-camera sensor fusion offers a promising solution to overcome
these challenges. Marine radar operates day or night and in fog, rain, or glare where optical cameras often
fail, and can detect objects at long range beyond the visible horizon. A preliminary sketch of this proposal
is seen in Figure 19.

Figure 19: Comparison of Camera and Radar detection systems
By leveraging radar’s all-weather detections to cue the camera, we can effectively “seed” the
image labeling process. For instance, if the radar returns indicate a target at a certain range and bearing,
that can flag a region-of-interest in the corresponding camera frame for a human annotator (or an assisting
algorithm) to verify and label. Researchers have begun exploring such cross-sensor cueing; for example, a
Radar Region Proposal Network (RRPN) has been proposed to project radar detections into the camera
view as candidate object regions. In our context, the radar will act as an automatic proposal generator for
the camera, focusing attention on likely object locations, especially valuable when objects are small or
visibility is poor. The overarching goal (and motivation for this project) is to bridge the radar and optical
modalities to overcome their individual limitations. A fusion of radar and camera information promises
more robust detection of small maritime objects and a means to automate data labeling. Radar can

Part 2 Technical Background
Part 2.1 Marine Radar Fundamentals
Modern marine radars operate using either a pulsed or Frequency-Modulated Continuous-Wave (FMCW)
scheme. Traditional pulsed radar (often magnetron-based) transmits high-power microwave pulses and
measures the echo return time to determine target range. These can emit kilowatt-level peak power pulses,
offering long-range detection, but they require a warm-up period and have a short blind zone (minimum
range) during each pulse when the radar cannot receive. In contrast, FMCW radar continuously sweeps its
transmit frequency and listens for the frequency-shifted returns, achieving similar range performance with
much lower peak power (on the order of tens of watts). An example of the difference between FMCW and
Pulse radar is shown in Figure 21. FMCW radars have no inherent blind range (since they transmit and
receive simultaneously) and provide improved short-range detection and Doppler sensitivity due to their
coherent processing. While magnetron pulse radars remain popular for their simplicity and long-range
capability, solid-state FMCW radars are increasingly adopted for their instant-on operation, lower power
consumption, and ability to detect very close-in objects that pulsed radars might miss.

Page 16 of 55
DISTRIBUTION STATEMENT D

Figure 21: Comparison of Pulse and FMCW Radar Waveforms
For this project, an X-band marine radar (9.4 GHz) is utilized due to low-cost, power efficiency, and easeof-use. Regardless of the chosen architecture, understanding radar operating mode (pulsed vs. FMCW) will
inform how collected data is to be processed and integrated with other sensors or cameras. Although some
radar systems (automotive primarily) are stationary; the majority of marine radar provides a 360° panoramic
image of echoes (often referred to as PPI or Plan Position Indicator) in which targets are detected against
sea clutter.
Part 2.2 Gain Control and CFAR
A key challenge in radar processing is distinguishing small targets from sea clutter and noise, which requires
careful setting of detection thresholds. Radar units include gain control circuits to adjust the amplification
of incoming echoes, effectively controlling the display intensity of returns. Proper gain setting is crucial:
set the gain too low and weak targets disappear; set it too high and the display saturates with background
noise or sea clutter. On older or lower-end radars, the gain is adjusted manually by the operator; typically
increasing it until noise speckles just become visible, then dialing back slightly. Skilled operators constantly
tweak the gain to maximize target visibility without overwhelming clutter. In heavy clutter (e.g. high sea
state or rain), an operator may reduce gain to avoid false returns, at the cost of possibly losing very small
echoes. A simple example of high and medium clutter environments is shown in Figure 22. A can be seen
in Figure 22, although a gain setting can work in one environment, it does not necessarily guarantee it will
work in another environment. Thus the major drawback of manual tuning is it’s subjectivity and inclination
towards continuous readjustment.

Figure 22: High and Medium Clutter Environments with Varying Gain Levels
Modern radar systems(such as the commercial ones utilized for this project) often employ Automatic Gain
Control (AGC) to continually adapt receiver gain based on overall return levels. AGC will lower gain when
the radar sees a lot of strong returns (for instance, large waves or nearby land) to prevent the display from
washing out, and raise gain in calmer conditions to maintain sensitivity. This adaptive approach keeps the
output within an optimal range without operator intervention. However, AGC is a blunt instrument; it does
not discriminate between wanted targets and unwanted clutter. In the presence of heavy clutter, AGC may
inadvertently suppress small target echoes along with the background, since it simply equalizes the total
output level. Thus, a tiny kayak could be damped out when large waves are in the vicinity, as the radar
autonomously reduces gain to handle the clutter. Finding the right balance is difficult, which is one reason
we are motivated to handle raw radar data in a more intelligent way (using sensor fusion and learning, as
described later) rather than relying purely on built-in AGC.

Another fundamental radar processing technique is the Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) detector. The
elementary basics of CFAR is shown graphically in Figure 24 while a version of CA-CFAR is shown on
real data in Figure 23. CFAR adaptively sets the detection threshold based on local clutter statistics to
maintain a roughly constant false alarm probability. In practice, the radar computes the average or
distribution of the return power in a sliding window around a cell and chooses a threshold such that, say,
only one in a million noise cells exceeds it (for a false alarm rate of 10 –6). Any echo stronger than this
threshold is declared a target. CFAR is very useful in maritime radar since sea clutter is not uniform: it
raises thresholds in regions of heavy backscatter (e.g. breaking waves) and lowers thresholds in calmer
areas. This adaptivity prevents a flood of false alarms that a fixed threshold would trigger in rough seas,
and conversely, it retains sensitivity in calm waters where a fixed high threshold would miss targets.

Page 19 of 55

Figure 24: CFAR 1D and 2D on Sample Radar Data
In addition to requiring tuning for each radar, CFAR is not foolproof. In intense, non-homogeneous clutter,
a small target’s echo may be submerged below a heightened local threshold; the radar effectively raises the
bar so high (to keep false alarms down) that the target is missed. For example, a slow-moving person in
water surrounded by choppy waves might be invisible to a standard CFAR detector because the local sea
clutter drives the threshold above the person’s return level. On the flip side, in non-homogenous clutter
scenarios, CFAR can become overly sensitive, requiring careful tuning of the false-alarm rate to avoid
peppering the screen with false targets. These limitations motivate exploring machine learning approaches,
which can potentially learn to recognize the subtle signal of small objects within clutter and motivate using
additional sensor modalities (camera) to confirm which radar returns are real targets.
Part 2.3 Planned Sensor Fusion Approach
By combining radar and camera data, it is believed there is a path to capitalize on the strengths of each. The
radar provides range, bearing, and a detection confidence (echo amplitude) for objects, even in darkness or
haze. The camera provides rich appearance information to classify and localize those objects in image
space. A critical step in fusion is sensor co-registration; i.e. calibrating the coordinate frames of the radar
and camera so that we can map a radar detection (range θ, bearing φ) to the corresponding pixel location in
the camera image. A calibration technique has not been settled upon yet, but will likely be of the
autonomous variety.
Once this calibration is in place, each radar detection can be projected into the camera view as an annotated
bounding box or marker. This allows a few immediate benefits: (1) Improved detection - if the radar detects
something that the camera-based algorithm missed, the system can direct attention to that region in the
image (for a potential false-negative recovery); and (2) Assisted labeling - as we begin building a training
dataset for camera object detection, the radar can pre-suggest regions of interest, drastically reducing the
human effort needed to scan each frame for tiny objects. In essence, the radar acts as an “extra pair of eyes”
for the camera, especially in scenarios of poor visibility or tiny targets. The aim is to implement a radarassisted labeling pipeline where radar detections provide initial bounding boxes for objects in the camera
images. A human annotator (or an automatic verification step) would then confirm or adjust these boxes,

resulting in labeled images with significantly less manual labor. A simple diagram with real sensor data
overlayed by a possible implementation for human annotators is shown in Figure 25. This approach
addresses the aforementioned labeling bottleneck by focusing human attention on likely object locations
instead of erratically and aimlessly searching large empty frames for small objects.

Part 2.4 Multi-Gain Radar Data for CNN
A novel aspect of the approach is how Radar gain is handled. As discussed, a single fixed gain (or even
AGC) can either wash out weak targets or flood the display with clutter. Our solution posits to capture
multiple radar intensity frames at different gain levels and feed them together into a neural network,
allowing the network to learn a composite understanding of the scene. The marine radar we use can be
configured to produce such multi-gain outputs via low-level manipulation of the radar software, and
utilizing temporal-based techniques. By doing so, the chosen detection architecture can avoid committing
to a single threshold early on. If the gain is too low, one frame will show almost nothing – even strong
returns like land might barely appear. Conversely, if the gain is too high, another frame will show everything
lit up (strong targets and noise speckle everywhere) so that true targets are hard to distinguish. In practice
today, an operator must manually adjust gain to find a Goldilocks setting for each situation. To reduce this
dependence on an expert user, we propose an automatic multi-gain fusion. Because the radar display’s
native output tends to be binary (see Figure 26) at any given gain, effectively an aggressive thresholding
that yields mostly 0 or 1 in the pixel matrix, no single setting can capture all targets. By combining multiple
“exposures” of the radar at low, medium, and high gain, it is possible to obtain a richer representation of
the underlying signal strength.

Page 21 of 55

Figure 26: Histograms of Different Gain Settings – 4-bit Quantized
The neural network will receive a stack of these frames (e.g. as separate input channels), from which it can
deduce that an object which is invisible in the low-gain frame but pops out in the high-gain frame is likely
a small target in heavy clutter, whereas something visible in low gain but blurred in high gain might be a
strong target or interference. Feeding multiple gain levels into a CNN is expected to improve small object
detection, essentially providing the network with dynamic range context that the radar’s own display
thresholding throws away. This idea is inspired by the concept of multi-exposure imaging in photography,
where combining images taken at different exposures can reveal details in both dark and bright regions. In
our case, the “exposures” are radar gains. An initial convolutional model (e.g. a custom ConvNet or an
adaptation of YOLO for radar) will be developed to take these multi-gain radar frames and output detections
(range, angle) of potential targets. Those detections can then be mapped onto the camera images as
described. Ultimately, the output of the fusion system will be both a radar PPI with target markers and
camera frames with annotated objects; a comprehensive situational picture. Preliminary results for gaincombining and clutter removal under High clutter simulated settings is shown in Figure 27 with somewhat
promising results.

Distance Cutoff
A major improvement in the orthorectification pipeline during this period involved switching from a fixed
image point cropping method to one based on a world coordinate distance cutoff. This change ensures that
rectification does not attempt to map pixels that are projected far from the image plane like points near the
horizon. Before, this would lead to rectification “to infinity” giving memory allocation errors, but this fixes
this problem without having to evaluate every image for the best distance to cut the images.
The process works by iterating along the edge pixels of the undistorted image and calculating the world
coordinates of each pixel until the world coordinate value is greater than the desired cutoff distance. As the
pitch angle increases, the number of pixels cut off the image is less. Once the pixel at the given distance
along the edge is determined it is used as one of the 4 corner points for image warping. Using these points,
the image is warped to world coordinates. This process is shown in Figure 35.

Radar-Camera-INS Integration on Jetson AGX Orin

Part 1: Introduction
During this work period, the JetSeaAI SIMRAD Halo24 SDK and its ROS2 driver were enhanced on the
Jetson AGX Orin to enable persistent recording of raw radar data. The purpose of producing timestamped
radar logs is to align each 360-degree sweep with camera imagery and INS metadata for downstream sensor
fusion analysis. To achieve this, the SDK was modified to intercept the User Datagram Protocol (UDP)
sector packets as soon as the SIMRAD initializes. The ROS2 node decodes each packet into a discrete
spoke, comprised of 1024 echo values at 0.175781-degree resolution. These spokes are buffered until 2048
of them (one full 360-degree rotation) have been assembled. Upon completion of each rotation, the node
saves the buffer to the Orin as a txt file. Each file contains microsecond-precision timestamps, sweep length,
azimuth angle, GUI-configured range scale, and all echo intensity values. Files are organized in uniquely
named, timestamped directories on the Orin. Please see Figure 36 for the radar data saving workflow. This
workflow provides lossless, rotation-aligned files that ensure precise time synchronization between radar,
camera, and INS data.

- Echo intensities: 1024 values (0-15) indicating return strength for each sample.

Rain Segmentation Model Improvements

Part 1: Training a rain segmentation model
We trained a rain segmentation model based on the U-Net architecture for rain segmentation in images.
The model takes a 352x352 rain image and returns a rain mask of the image as shown in Figure 41. The
model has 4 encoder blocks, and 4 decoder blocks similar to a traditional U-Net [1, 2]. The key innovation
of this model is the pretrained Hiera block in the encoder block of the model. The Hiera architecture is a
hierarchical vision transformer that outputs four different channel resolution (144, 288, 576, 1152) and is
from the pretrained state-of-the-art Segment Anything Model 2 [3]. All the components of the model are
trained except the Hiera block. The weights of the Hiera block are frozen because we utilize the pretrained
weights which is more memory-efficient than training from scratch. An adapter layer is added before each
Hiera block to ensure efficient fine-tuning and encoding the features from the rain data since the Hiera
block has not been trained on any rain data [2]. The adapter contains a downscale, activation function
(GeLU), upscale, and GeLU to sufficiently learn to encode the features of the input image. After each
encoder block the height and width resolution of the image are halved (88, 44, 22, 11) while the channels
are doubled (144, 288, 576, 1152). Aggregating these multi-channel tensors is difficult which is why
receptive field blocks (RFBs) are used to enhance the features by having a fixed channel size of 64. The
neck of the model outputs a 64x11x11 tensor which is upscaled and concatenated with the previous RFB
output of dimension 64x22x22. This is repeated until we have rain mask of the same resolution as the input
image.

Page 35 of 55

The model uses a weighted IoU loss and binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss across the three different rain
mask outputs of the model. The model hyperparameters include a batch size of 16, learning rate of 0.0002,
AdamW optimizer, and cosine annealing learning rate scheduler. The AdamW optimizer is used for
finetuning transformers and the cosine annealing learning rate scheduler adjusts the learning rate based on
a cosine curve. The scheduler starts with a high learning rate, the learning rate is gradually reduced
following a cosine function, and then it restarts back at a high learning rate (warm restart) [4]. The model

Geometric Range Estimation

Last month, we investigated the effect that camera calibration has on geometric range estimation.
Furthermore, we also investigated the effect the IMU (and subsequently when we pull the IMU value) has
on range estimation. This month we continued the investigation into calibration and made significant
progress in improving the calibration of our perception systems. We successfully tested and evaluated
multiple calibration methods, gaining valuable insights into how each approach impacts system
performance. Notably, we implemented a joint calibration procedure for the camera and IMU, obtaining
both a measured transformation matrix and a time offset between the two sensors.
Over the course of the month, we conducted four distinct calibration methods: close, mid, far, and
combined.
 Close calibration (0-5m) involved placing the calibration board a few meters from the camera and
moving the camera (left, right, and tilted) to gather sufficient IMU variation.
 Mid calibration (5-10m) followed a similar procedure, with the board positioned approximately
five meters away.
 Far calibration (10-15m) extended the distance to around ten meters, again with camera motion
to introduce variability.
 Combined calibration kept the camera stationary while the target was moved between close, mid,
and far positions, including tilts.
Images collected from these sessions were processed using Kalibr, and their intrinsic parameters and
distortion coefficients were compared. Among the four methods, far calibration produced the lowest
reprojection error. We suspect this is due to the calibration board’s pattern being smaller in the image at
greater distances, providing a more accurate representation of the scene. This result suggests that board
scale and target distance may play a significant role in calibration quality, which we plan to explore further
in our ongoing work to refine the calibration procedure for minimal reprojection error. Previous work on
this contract for Task 1.4 on uncertainty suggests we may need to consider longer ranges (possibly as long
as 20 meters) to ensure we are conducting the calibration at ranges where the factors that influence the lens
modulation transfer function (MTF) have stabilized.
Additionally, we successfully incorporated the IMU into our system calibration. Using Kalibr, we first
calibrated the camera alone, then performed a joint calibration with the IMU. This process yielded a
Page 40 of 55

transformation matrix that accounts for the positional and rotational offsets between the camera and IMU,
improving the accuracy of sensor fusion tasks such as ray tracing. These small offsets become increasingly

Longwave Infrared (LWIR) Range Estimation

Last month, the team trained the Infrared Ranging Algorithm, XGBoost, with 269 LWIR images
paired with ground truth distance datapoints. The training variables included the x-pixel of the object, the
y-pixel of the object, and the difference in intensity of water particles near the object and water particles
near the sensor. Due to initial parameterization and lack of sensor gain as a training variable, the
algorithm did not demonstrate any robustness to variability in environment. The infrared ranging
algorithm performed decently when trained and tested in comparable environments; however, the
algorithm performed poorly when it was trained and tested with datapoints that combined mid-day,
sunny, cloudy, and night-time scenes.
This month, Integer explored methods to increase the robustness and reliability of the infrared
ranging algorithm. The primary theory for the lack of robustness in the algorithm was because the LWIR
sensors that collected the open-source data utilized Automatic Gain Control (AGC). AGC directly
impacted the fluctuation of intensity values within each image from scene to scene based on ambient
environment temperature ranges, regardless of the actual temperature of the water particles. In the middle
of the day, the AGC caused water particles to have a much smaller change in pixel intensity as a function
of distance. Conversely, in the evening, the AGC caused water particles to have a larger pixel intensity
difference as a function of distance. This was due to a larger range of received infrared radiation during
the middle of the day, where the sun is heating objects in the background to a much higher temperature.
Not knowing the value of the sensor gain in each image made it impossible for XGBoost to accurately
predict the range to an object based solely on the pixel intensity difference and x/y-pixel location of
object in-frame. Utilizing AGC in an infrared ranging algorithm is only a viable method if the gain value
is quantified and parameterized into the algorithm. Shown below in Figure 44 is a visualization of the
impact that AGC has on pixel intensity gradient of the sea surface from two vastly different scenes.

estimation (MDE) problem in open-world scenes.
Monocular depth estimation is a computer vision task that aims to predict the depth of objects in a
scene. When working with models which aim to solve this problem there are generally two types of
solutions: absolute depth and relative depth. Relative depth is the most common approach. Relative refers
to the ratio between two points, the model determines the result based on comparison between different
reference points. The absolute, also known as metric depth estimation, determines the depth of the object
on a physical scale, normally the metric system. The model used for this investigation was DepthAnything-v2 [5]. Integer was particularly interested in this model primarily because of their initial fully
synthetic high accuracy training schema and their label-level distillation technique using pseudo labels.
The model encoder is first trained on purely synthetic data with high-accuracy and high-granularity
labels. Next, the trained encoder is used to create labels of real images, and the student model is then
trained on these pseudo-labels. An example of the training pipeline is shown in Figure 46 and some
examples of the model performing in an indoor environment are shown in Figure 47.

2.2.10 Shoreline Characterization
This month, Integer began an investigation on potential shoreline surveillance approaches with the
goal of autonomously making go/no-go decisions about beach landings. In shoreline characterization for
beach landings, image segmentation is a promising approach that can be used to accurately identify
known objects and potential hazards that appear on the shore zone. The pixel level detail from
segmentation [7] can accurately map densely packed hazards, such as rocks [8] or vegetation [9]. Fine
grained knowledge of shoreline composition via segmentation is valuable for assessing safe beach landing
zones.

Page 49 of 55
DISTRIBUTION STATEMENT D

The ideal segmentation model balances accuracy, efficiency, and hardware constraints. Models like
SAM2 [10], SegFormer [11], and YOLOv7 [12] are state of the art segmentation models that offer
different strengths. At a high level, SAM2 (Segment Anything v2) provides high quality, prompt-based
segmentation. An example of a SAM2 inference is shown in Figure 50. Despite achieving strong
shoreline segmentation in this example, the base model was found to be computationally heavy, making it
less practical for real time inferencing on lightweight edge devices. On the other hand, YOLOv7 for
segmentation offers fast inference with strong performance and continues to be a reliable semantic
segmentation model to use, but the advent of transformers has made the YOLOv7 architecture slightly
dated. In contrast, SegFormer, is a lightweight transformer-based model designed by NVIDIA for
semantic segmentation [13]. It notably has a strong balance between speed, accuracy, and resource
efficiency, making it particularly well suited for shoreline segmentation tasks, specifically on NVIDIA
hardware. A performance plot in Figure 51 compares the mean Intersection of Union [14] (mIOU) metrics
obtained on the ADE20k dataset [15] from various segmentation models, such as SegFormer, DeepLab
[16], FCN [17], and Swin transformers [18]. The figure highlights that SegFormer is shown to achieve the
highest performance on the ADE20k dataset (51.0% mIOU) while also having significantly less
parameters than other models. This, in tandem with intrinsic compatibility with NVIDIA devices, makes
SegFormer a good starting model to work with.
To train a SegFormer model for shoreline hazard segmentation, a segmentation dataset containing
shoreline data is needed. While ocean related imagery is widely available online, data specifically
depicting a boat approaching a shoreline, from the perspective of the boat, is scarce. This gap between
what is needed and what is openly available highlights the value of designing a custom, ocean world
environment using a video game engine. A virtual ocean environment was created on Blender [19], with a
camera attached to the simulated boat. An example image captured from this scene is shown in Figure 52.
In this example, the boat was animated to follow controlled paths, effectively simulating real world
scenarios (e.g., a boat moving towards the shore). By extracting individual frames and generating binary
masks for each object layer, a large, customizable segmentation dataset can be created. Examples of these
binary masks are shown in Figure 53. These masks form highly accurate, pixel level annotations that
simulate complex beach landing scenarios, such as the presence of hazards on the beach. This synthetic
dataset fills a crucial gap in open-source shoreline data and ultimately enables precise training and
benchmarking of segmentation models in realistic, but controlled conditions.
In conclusion, a Blender-based shoreline simulation has enabled the creation of a tailored, boatapproaching-shoreline segmentation dataset that addresses a critical gap in open-sourced training data for
real time shoreline characterization. This simulation-to-annotation pipeline has laid the groundwork for
training efficient segmentation models like SegFormer. Future work will focus on generating a highfidelity, Blender sourced segmentation dataset that simulates a shoreline approach scenario from the
perspective of a boat mounted camera. This dataset will include diverse terrain types such as sand, rocks,
vegetation, or debris, with segmentation style annotations for each frame. This will be used to later
evaluate the performance of the SegFormer model in detecting and segmenting shoreline hazards.
