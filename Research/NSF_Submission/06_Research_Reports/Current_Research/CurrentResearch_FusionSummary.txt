Short summary: Develop a fusion-based approach to reliably detect small, low-RCS objects at sea by
combining radar and camera data, and use radar cues to streamline image labeling.
Summary
Conventional marine radars alone struggle to detect small, low radar-cross-section (RCS) objects (like
kayaks, swimmers, buoys, pylons) in higher sea states and heavy sea clutter, and optical camera detectors
are limited by scarce labeled data in marine settings. By fusing X-band radar and cameras, the system will
improve the detection of small maritime objects and automatically assist in labeling camera images using
radar returns as cues.

PART 1.

INTRODUCTION & BACKGROUND

In maritime settings, vision-based object detection faces a severe data labeling bottleneck. Building large,
annotated image datasets of small boats or floating objects at sea is expensive and time-consuming. Such
objects, as seen in Figure 1, are relatively rare and often only a few pixels in size, so collecting and labeling
examples requires expert annotators to sift through hours of mostly-empty footage.

Figure 1: Small Object Detection: A Succinct Review

Existing public maritime image datasets are few, and those that do exist have limited scale and
diversity. Deep learning models demand plentiful labeled examples to achieve high accuracy, yet acquiring
those examples on the open ocean is impractical. As a result, current vision models trained on sparse
maritime data struggle to detect small, distant objects reliably. In comparison to common object detection
on land, labeling maritime scenes (mostly water with tiny far-off targets) is far more costly and difficult.

Secondary sensor suites, such as marine radar systems, often have difficulty reliably detecting very
small, low-RCS targets (e.g. a partially submerged person, small buoy, or debris) against the dynamic ocean

radars (for example, airborne periscope-detection radars) suffer high false-alarm rates in choppy conditions,
as breaking wave tops produce spurious bright clutter spikes that can mimic or eclipse genuine targets. In
short, using radar alone, small maritime objects are often lost in clutter or generate many false detections.

PART 2.

TECHNICAL BACKGROUND

Part 2.1

Marine Radar Fundamentals

Modern marine radars operate using either a pulsed or Frequency-Modulated Continuous-Wave (FMCW)
scheme. Traditional pulsed radar (often magnetron-based) transmits high-power microwave pulses and
measures the echo return time to determine target range. These can emit kilowatt-level peak power pulses,
offering long-range detection, but they require a warm-up period and have a short blind zone (minimum
range) during each pulse when the radar cannot receive. In contrast, FMCW radar continuously sweeps its
transmit frequency and listens for the frequency-shifted returns, achieving similar range performance with
much lower peak power (on the order of tens of watts). An example of the difference between FMCW and
Pulse radar is shown in Figure 5. FMCW radars have no inherent blind range (since they transmit and
receive simultaneously) and provide improved short-range detection and Doppler sensitivity due to their
coherent processing. While magnetron pulse radars remain popular for their simplicity and long-range
capability, solid-state FMCW radars are increasingly adopted for their instant-on operation, lower power
consumption, and ability to detect very close-in objects that pulsed radars might miss.

Part 2.2

Gain Control and CFAR

A key challenge in radar processing is distinguishing small targets from sea clutter and noise, which requires
careful setting of detection thresholds. Radar units include gain control circuits to adjust the amplification
of incoming echoes, effectively controlling the display intensity of returns. Proper gain setting is crucial:
set the gain too low and weak targets disappear; set it too high and the display saturates with background
noise or sea clutter. On older or lower-end radars, the gain is adjusted manually by the operator; typically
increasing it until noise speckles just become visible, then dialing back slightly. Skilled operators constantly
tweak the gain to maximize target visibility without overwhelming clutter. In heavy clutter (e.g. high sea
state or rain), an operator may reduce gain to avoid false returns, at the cost of possibly losing very small
echoes. A simple example of high and medium clutter environments is shown in Figure 6. A can be seen
in Figure 6, although a gain setting can work in one environment, it does not necessarily guarantee it will
work in another environment. Thus the major drawback of manual tuning is it’s subjectivity and inclination
towards continuous readjustment.

Modern radar systems(such as the commercial ones utilized for this project) often employ
Automatic Gain Control (AGC) to continually adapt receiver gain based on overall return levels. AGC will
lower gain when the radar sees a lot of strong returns (for instance, large waves or nearby land) to prevent
the display from washing out, and raise gain in calmer conditions to maintain sensitivity. This adaptive
approach keeps the output within an optimal range without operator intervention. However, AGC is a blunt
instrument; it does not discriminate between wanted targets and unwanted clutter. In the presence of heavy
clutter, AGC may inadvertently suppress small target echoes along with the background, since it simply
equalizes the total output level. Thus, a tiny kayak could be damped out when large waves are in the vicinity,
as the radar autonomously reduces gain to handle the clutter. Finding the right balance is difficult, which is
one reason we are motivated to handle raw radar data in a more intelligent way (using sensor fusion and
learning, as described later) rather than relying purely on built-in AGC.

Another fundamental radar processing technique is the Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR)
detector. The elementary basics of CFAR is shown graphically in Figure 8 while a version of CA-CFAR is
shown on real data in Figure 7. CFAR adaptively sets the detection threshold based on local clutter statistics
to maintain a roughly constant false alarm probability. In practice, the radar computes the average or
distribution of the return power in a sliding window around a cell and chooses a threshold such that, say,
only one in a million noise cells exceeds it (for a false alarm rate of 10 –6). Any echo stronger than this
threshold is declared a target. CFAR is very useful in maritime radar since sea clutter is not uniform: it
raises thresholds in regions of heavy backscatter (e.g. breaking waves) and lowers thresholds in calmer
areas. This adaptivity prevents a flood of false alarms that a fixed threshold would trigger in rough seas,
and conversely, it retains sensitivity in calm waters where a fixed high threshold would miss targets.

In addition to requiring tuning for each radar, CFAR is not foolproof. In intense, non-homogeneous
clutter, a small target’s echo may be submerged below a heightened local threshold; the radar effectively
raises the bar so high (to keep false alarms down) that the target is missed. For example, a slow-moving
person in water surrounded by choppy waves might be invisible to a standard CFAR detector because the
local sea clutter drives the threshold above the person’s return level. On the flip side, in non-homogenous
clutter scenarios, CFAR can become overly sensitive, requiring careful tuning of the false-alarm rate to
avoid peppering the screen with false targets. These limitations motivate exploring machine learning
approaches, which can potentially learn to recognize the subtle signal of small objects within clutter and
motivate using additional sensor modalities (camera) to confirm which radar returns are real targets.

Part 2.3

Planned Sensor Fusion Approach

By combining radar and camera data, it is believed there is a path to capitalize on the strengths of each. The
radar provides range, bearing, and a detection confidence (echo amplitude) for objects, even in darkness or
haze. The camera provides rich appearance information to classify and localize those objects in image
space. A critical step in fusion is sensor co-registration; i.e. calibrating the coordinate frames of the radar
and camera so that we can map a radar detection (range θ, bearing φ) to the corresponding pixel location in
the camera image. A calibration technique has not been settled upon yet, but will likely be of the
autonomous variety.
Once this calibration is in place, each radar detection can be projected into the camera view as an
annotated bounding box or marker. This allows a few immediate benefits: (1) Improved detection - if the
radar detects something that the camera-based algorithm missed, the system can direct attention to that
region in the image (for a potential false-negative recovery); and (2) Assisted labeling - as we begin building
a training dataset for camera object detection, the radar can pre-suggest regions of interest, drastically
reducing the human effort needed to scan each frame for tiny objects. In essence, the radar acts as an “extra
pair of eyes” for the camera, especially in scenarios of poor visibility or tiny targets. The aim is to implement
a radar-assisted labeling pipeline where radar detections provide initial bounding boxes for objects in the

Part 2.4

Multi-Gain Radar Data for CNN

A novel aspect of the approach is how Radar gain is handled. As discussed, a single fixed gain (or even
AGC) can either wash out weak targets or flood the display with clutter. Our solution posits to capture
multiple radar intensity frames at different gain levels and feed them together into a neural network,
allowing the network to learn a composite understanding of the scene. The marine radar we use can be
configured to produce such multi-gain outputs via low-level manipulation of the radar software, and
utilizing temporal-based techniques. By doing so, the chosen detection architecture can avoid committing
to a single threshold early on. If the gain is too low, one frame will show almost nothing – even strong
returns like land might barely appear. Conversely, if the gain is too high, another frame will show everything
lit up (strong targets and noise speckle everywhere) so that true targets are hard to distinguish. In practice
today, an operator must manually adjust gain to find a Goldilocks setting for each situation. To reduce this
dependence on an expert user, we propose an automatic multi-gain fusion. Because the radar display’s
native output tends to be binary (see Figure 10) at any given gain, effectively an aggressive thresholding
that yields mostly 0 or 1 in the pixel matrix, no single setting can capture all targets. By combining multiple
“exposures” of the radar at low, medium, and high gain, it is possible to obtain a richer representation of
the underlying signal strength.

The neural network will receive a stack of these frames (e.g. as separate input channels), from
which it can deduce that an object which is invisible in the low-gain frame but pops out in the high-gain
frame is likely a small target in heavy clutter, whereas something visible in low gain but blurred in high
gain might be a strong target or interference. Feeding multiple gain levels into a CNN is expected to improve
small object detection, essentially providing the network with dynamic range context that the radar’s own
display thresholding throws away. This idea is inspired by the concept of multi-exposure imaging in
photography, where combining images taken at different exposures can reveal details in both dark and
bright regions. In our case, the “exposures” are radar gains. An initial convolutional model (e.g. a custom
ConvNet or an adaptation of YOLO for radar) will be developed to take these multi-gain radar frames and
output detections (range, angle) of potential targets. Those detections can then be mapped onto the camera
images as described. Ultimately, the output of the fusion system will be both a radar PPI with target markers
and camera frames with annotated objects; a comprehensive situational picture. Preliminary results for
gain-combining and clutter removal under High clutter simulated settings is shown in Figure 11 with
somewhat promising results.

components and milestones of this research ‘phase’ are as follow below.

Part 3.2

Collect Pilot Dataset

Conduct data collection trials to gather a pilot dataset of synchronized radar and camera recordings. The

Part 3.3

Multi-Gain Data Fusion Technique

Implement the scheme of generating multi-gain radar input frames. We will produce, for example, three
intensity images: one with low gain (conservative threshold), one medium, and one high gain (sensitive).
These three images form a multi-channel radar representation of that moment. This step may involve
developing a script or using the radar’s SDK to adjust gain or threshold levels. We will validate that this
approach indeed highlights targets across the frames, e.g. a small kayak might only appear in the high-gain
image, whereas land or large vessels appear even in low-gain. The combination effectively spans the
dynamic range of returns.
Part 3.4

CNN Model Development

Design and train a baseline convolutional neural network that ingests the multi-gain radar data (and
potentially temporal sequences) to detect objects. As a starting point, we plan to implement a 1 D
convolutional detector that scans each radar range-azimuth slice (similar to a Conv1D over the radar’s polar
coordinate image). This simple model will be trained to distinguish target returns from clutter using the
pilot data. We will compare its performance to the classical 1D CA-CFAR detector as a baseline. The
hypothesis is that even a basic learning-based model can achieve better sensitivity than CFAR for low-RCS
targets, when provided with multi-gain inputs. As we iterate, more sophisticated models (e.g. 2 D CNN on

Part 3.5

End-to-End Detection Pipeline

Integrate the radar-CNN and camera. Detected target coordinates from the radar-CNN will be mapped onto
the camera frame in real-time (or offline, initially). We will implement a simple user interface to visualize
results – for example, drawing radar detections as bounding boxes on the camera video. This will serve as
a radar-assisted labeling tool: when we pause the video, the system can output suggested annotations for
any objects the radar picked up. The pipeline will be evaluated on the pilot dataset by measuring detection
rates and correctness of the suggested labels.

Part 3.6

Preliminary Evaluation and Iteration

Evaluate the performance of the radar-based detector versus traditional methods. Specifically, we will
compare detection outcomes of our CNN to the radar’s standard ARPA implementation. Key metrics
include probability of detection for the small test targets and false alarm rates. If the initial model
underperforms in some scenarios (e.g. very rough clutter), we will analyze the failure cases and consider
additional inputs or model architecture changes. We expect to demonstrate improvement over CFAR, at
least in moderate conditions, as a proof-of-concept result suitable for publication.

Part 3.7

Radar-Assisted Image Labeling

As an offshoot of the detection pipeline, develop a semi-automatic labeling tool for the camera imagery.
This tool will use the radar detections (after filtering out obvious false alarms) to mark regions in each video
frame that likely contain an object. A human annotator can then quickly confirm the presence of an object
and label its bounding box or class, rather than searching from scratch. We will apply this process to our
pilot dataset to generate training labels for the camera. This not only accelerates data annotation but also
yields a more diverse training set of small-object images than could reasonably be labeled manually. The
effectiveness of radar cueing will be quantified by measuring the reduction in manual effort (e.g. how many
proposals per hour the radar provides and what fraction are true objects). This addresses the objective of an
automated labeling pipeline using radar cues.

EXPECTED RESULTS & OUTCOMES

Improving the detection of small objects in the maritime domain has significant real-world implications.
From a maritime safety perspective, the ability to spot a person overboard or a semi-submerged hazard (e.g.
a floating log or container) can save lives and prevent collisions. Small vessels such as kayaks or dinghies
are often missed by large ship radars until dangerously close; a better detection system could alert operators
or autonomous navigation systems earlier.
Enhancing radar detectability of these objects – without overwhelming false alarms – fills a critical
gap in surveillance of ports, coastal waters, and open oceans. This is increasingly important as we move
toward unmanned surface vessels and advanced maritime traffic monitoring, where reliable automation of
small object detection is required for safety and security. The fusion approach in this project also offers a
valuable benefit for data generation and algorithm development.
By using radar to assist in labeling optical imagery, we tackle the data scarcity issue that plagues
maritime computer vision. Instead of relying on hundreds of hours of tedious manual annotation to train a
camera-based detector, the radar’s cues allow us to rapidly label a high-quality dataset. This radar-assisted
labeling not only reduces human workload dramatically, but it also improves dataset quality; the labels are
more likely to catch hard-to-see objects that a human might overlook when fatigued. In essence, our
approach leverages the complementary nature of the sensors: radar provides precision in detection cueing,
and the camera provides rich detail for classification. Together, they yield a system more robust than either
alone, particularly for the challenging maritime environment.
PART 5.

FUTURE DIRECTION

This first phase of research will establish a strong foundation for a longer-term Ph.D. roadmap. In the future,
we plan to build on the prototype and concepts developed during the Master’s work to tackle deeper
challenges in radar perception and sensor fusion. Some future research directions include:
Part 5.1

Self-Supervised Radar Representation Learning

I will explore training the radar detection models with self-supervised learning techniques to reduce reliance
on extensive labeled data. One planned approach is a Radar Masked Autoencoder (Radar-MAE), where the
model learns to reconstruct missing portions of radar scans, thereby learning robust features from raw data.
By pre-training on a large amount of unlabeled radar video (tens or hundreds of hours), the model can
develop an understanding of sea clutter characteristics and typical target signatures. These features can then
be fine-tuned for detection tasks with relatively few labeled examples (few-shot learning). This thrust is
expected to lead to a conference publication (e.g. CVPR ’27 or ‘28) showcasing that a self-supervised radar
model outperforms a fully supervised one given limited labels.

Part 5.2

Physics-Informed and Continual Learning

Another future direction is to incorporate radar signal processing physics into the neural network
architecture. For example, embedding the radar’s known range equation or CFAR principles as
differentiable components (loss terms or network layers) could guide the model to make physically
plausible detections (controlling false alarm rates explicitly). We will also investigate Vision Transformer
(ViT) models tailored to radar by encoding priors like the pulse repetition frequency and antenna patterns.
In tandem, as we deploy the system in varied environments, continual learning strategies will be needed so
the model can adapt to new sea states or sensor configurations without forgetting old knowledge.
Techniques like online domain adaptation and Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) are planned to ensure
the radar perception system remains robust over time. These advanced topics form the core of much later
work, aiming for journal-level contributions in the radar/remote sensing field.
Part 5.3

A-MARV Dataset Expansion

Throughout the entire project, we expect to significantly scale up our data collection to build the Amplitudeonly Maritime Radar Video (A-MARV) dataset; the first open benchmark dataset of its kind. The full AMARV will comprise many hours of real radar recordings (X-band, ~9.4 GHz) captured in diverse
conditions, using a tri-gain recording strategy (temporal low/medium/high gain frames) to preserve smalltarget visibility. It is planned to label a portion of this data with “strong” annotations (definite object labels)
and an even larger portion with “weak” annotations generated by semi-automatic means (e.g. the radarassisted labeling plus a data programming approach using tools like Snorkel). The A-MARV dataset will
be published with standardized tasks (detection, domain adaptation, continual learning, few-shot learning)
to catalyze research in maritime radar ML. The goal is to eventually release A-MARV along with baseline
models and an evaluation toolkit, establishing a community resource. This will not only validate the broader
impact of our work but also ensure reproducibility and benchmarking for future researchers. A dedicated
data descriptor paper (e.g. in IEEE T-GRS) is planned once the initial version of the dataset is ready.
