Dear Members of the Evaluation Committee,

I enthusiastically recommend Noah Young for the NASA SC Space Grant Undergraduate Research Award. As his faculty advisor in the (iMSEL) Integrated Multiphysics and Systems Engineering Laboratory, since August 2025, I have supervised his research on radar and autonomous perception systems from his first few weeks of college. 

Over my career, I have supervised dozens of undergraduate researchers and among all undergraduates, Noah's output places him solidly above average. Among freshmen, he is in the top quartile, and measured against the broader pool of students I have taught, he ranks in the top five percent.

Noah joined iMSEL at the start of his first semester as an Honors College aerospace engineering student. He is now contributing to a planned ASME IDETC 2026 submission on synthetic radar data augmentation, in which he developed a method using land/water segmentation and object extraction to regenerate radar imagery from real sensor data.

When he began, radar fundamentals (signal geometry, terrain interaction, image processing) were almost entirely new to him. Rather than waiting for instruction, he independently read background literature on radar captures, pulse compression radar, and geometric simplification methods, then applied what he learned to produce working code within weeks. 

This self-directed learning style is characteristic of how he operates. Often he would rather teach himself a topic first and arrive with informed questions than chat with a graduate student or peers on basics. Somewhat impressively, he has maintained a 4.0\,GPA while carrying this research workload.

His technical contributions span multiple systems in our lab, and another distinguishing feature of him is the level of initiative and polish he brings to each task. As a first task, he fine-tuned a YOLO model for maritime small-object detection and deployed it on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin -- beginning some of our earlier tests on real-time radar object detection. 

He additionally designed a 3D-printable radar antenna mount in CAD that enables our Furuno NXT radar to be integrated onto a research vessel for offshore data collection. He also built a raw radar-to-PNG conversion pipeline and the real-time visualization tools, in Rust, that the radar team now uses for post-processing workflows, especially for computer vision workflows.

In each case, he did not only complete the task he was assigned, he went above and beyond, identifying adjacent problems (file processing speed, export compatibility, visualization format) and solving them without being asked. He also puts more effort into the quality of his deliverables than many undergraduates who treat research as a job.

Noah delivers weekly technical presentations to the radar team and monthly written reports to external sponsors. For a freshman explaining radar signal processing to project managers who are not domain specialists, the level of structured communication is relatively uncommon. Most undergraduates at this stage can/only summarize what they did, but Noah is able to frame why it matters and what should come next.

His proposed project -- an open-source terrain-occluded radar simulator validated against measured returns -- grows directly from his current work building the radar data visualization and conversion tools. He has already demonstrated that he can take an unfamiliar domain (radar physics), learn it independently, and produce lab-infrastructure tools that the full team relies on. 

The proposed simulator extends this trajectory: he would move from visualizing real radar data to generating synthetic data and validating it against our existing offshore captures. I believe the project is scoped appropriately for an undergraduate researcher who has already proven he can deliver intermediate complexity, functioning software on schedule.

I wholeheartedly recommend Noah Young for this award.
