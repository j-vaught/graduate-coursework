Part 2: Gain Analysis and Collected Data Summary

Part 2a: Gain Analysis
Traditional High Dynamic Range (HDR) algorithms typically require sensor characterization to boost
dynamic range. This often involves comparing a sensor's response to a "ground truth" measurement.
However, when a ground truth isn't available, alternative approaches are necessary. For this
system, relative gain can be calculated using various mathematical and statistical methods.
To analyze the gain, the radar was configured for minimal signal processing. Features like interpolation,
RezBoost (Software-augmented Horizontal Beam Width), clutter control, and STC were all deactivated to
ensure the purest possible characterization of the radar itself. Data was collected across 5 different
ranges and 100 distinct gain values, with 5 samples per value. One sample set was discarded in each
setting due to mid-sweep settings changes.

Figure 1: Example of simple HDR via Stacking Frames

Figure 1 illustrates the concept of simple HDR by stacking three 4-bit frames to effectively
achieve 12 bits of dynamic range instead of 4. Notice that at high gain, distinguishing wave
reflectivity from objects becomes difficult. Conversely, at low gain, some objects are barely
detectable, and wave reflectivity is absent. The HDR stacked result effectively retains wave
information while still enabling clear visual detection of actual objects.
One crucial method for empirically determining optimal gain values in a radar system is through the
analysis of Shannon entropy. Due to the quantized nature of the radar's Analog-to-Digital Converter
(ADC) and the inherent probabilistic distribution of received radar signals (comprising targets, clutter,
and noise), Shannon entropy serves as a robust metric for quantifying the information content and
statistical complexity of the digitized radar returns.
Shannon entropy, denoted as H(X), is calculated based on the probability distribution of the discrete
amplitude values (xi) of the digitized radar signal. For a discrete random variable X with possible
outcomes {x1,x2,…,xn} and corresponding probabilities P(xi), the entropy is given by:

probability of observing a specific amplitude value xi from the digitized radar output. A higher entropy
value indicates a greater degree of uncertainty, randomness, or information within the signal, whereas
lower entropy suggests more predictability or less information.

Figure 2: Entropy rates vs Gain across different range settings

The results of the Shannon entropy analysis for varying gain settings are presented in Figure 2. The
observed relationship between gain and entropy can be segmented into four distinct zones, each indicating
the predominant source and characteristic of the observed radar signal:
1. Noise Floor Zone: This initial zone is characterized by a moderate, but generally low, increase in
entropy. At these minimal gain settings, the received signal is primarily dominated by the radar
receiver's internal thermal noise (e.g., Johnson-Nyquist noise). While some amplification of this
inherent system noise occurs, leading to a slight increase in signal variance and thus entropy, the
system is largely insensitive to external targets. Any detectable objects in this zone would typically be
close and possess a high Radar Cross Section (RCS). This phenomenon is evident in Figure 3.G1 and
Figure 3.G15, which show that while some returns exist, it predominantly originates from areas near
the radar or from targets with a high RCS.
2. Target Acquisition Zone: In this zone, the entropy exhibits a slow, steady increase with rising gain.
This indicates the radar system is transitioning into its effective detection range. Weak target echoes,
low-level ambient clutter (e.g., from ground or structures), and external environmental noise begin to
be amplified above the internal noise floor. The signal's statistical complexity grows as it starts to
incorporate contributions from these external sources. However, while most objects become

detectable, especially stronger ones, low-RCS targets or returns from less reflective materials like
water or light rain may still be under the minimum quantized value. This can be seen in Figure 3.G20
and Figure 3.G80, where most major objects are detectable across the entire range with minimal
clutter being introduced.
3. Information Rich Zone: This zone is marked by a dramatic increase in Shannon entropy. At these gain
settings, the radar achieves ‘optimal’ sensitivity, ensuring that a wide range of received signals,
including echoes from targets of varying RCS (even relatively low-RCS ones), significant clutter
returns, and amplified noise, are well above the system's noise floor. The signal becomes highly
complex and information-rich due to the superposition of these diverse and strong returns,
maximizing the potential for target detection. However, it is a fine balance, as excessive gain beyond
this point can lead to signal degradation. This is particularly evident in Figure 3.G85 and Figure
3.G93, Both images contain significant clutter, rain, and sea returns, indicating high information
density. However, objects become more easily washed out or blend into the background at these
higher gain settings compared to the previous zones.
4. Saturation Zone: Characterized by a sharp, often near-vertical, decrease in entropy at the highest gain
settings. This drastic drop is primarily due to the saturation and clipping of the radar's Analog-toDigital Converter (ADC) or preceding analog receiver stages. When the amplified signal exceeds the
maximum voltage or digital value the hardware can process, it becomes flattened at that limit. This
non-linear distortion reduces the true dynamic range of the signal, decreasing the number of unique
amplitude values and thereby making the signal statistically more predictable within its clipped range,
leading to a significant reduction in calculated entropy. In this zone, even moderate returns, such as
those from sea clutter or heavy rain, become fully saturated, rendering the signal severely
compromised and hindering effective target discrimination. As seen in Figure 3.G95 and Figure
3.G99, it's visually apparent that even sea and rain clutter are saturating the ADC, which results
in reduced overall information density.

Part 3: Furuno Radar Details & ARPA
Radar systems, like the Furuno unit(s) used in this study, operate by emitting electromagnetic
pulses and interpreting the reflected signals. The precision and utility of the data derived from
these systems are heavily influenced by the fundamental parameters of these pulses and the
subsequent signal processing. The ability to manipulate and understand these parameters is
crucial for achieving high dynamic range (HDR) and effective clutter removal, which are some
of the goals of this research.
Part 3a: FURUNO specs
The core of radar operation involves the transmission of energy in discrete bursts and the
measurement of returns. For the Furuno system in use, a single radial (a line extending outwards
from the radar's center) is composed of 868 ‘dots’ or range cells, representing individual range
measurements. A full 360-degree sweep is captured by approximately 600 radials in this system
with ‘high RPM’. However, with advanced functionalities such as Dual Pulse, this data density
can effectively be doubled to ~1200 radials per sweep, providing a significantly finer resolution

of the surrounding area. This dense data capture is critical for later computational analysis and
the application of machine learning models like the proposed MAE.

The choice of pulse length is critical for radar performance. As illustrated in Figure 9, Furuno
radars utilize various "chirp durations" or pulse lengths, each optimized for specific operational
tiers.
Shorter pulse lengths (S1, S2 - Harbor tiers) are ideal for close-range detection, offering high
range resolution to distinguish between closely spaced targets like buoys or other vessels in
congested waterways. The trade-off is typically lower overall power and thus shorter detection
range.
Medium pulse lengths (M1, M2, M3 - Coastal tiers) provide a balance between range resolution
and detection capability, suitable for navigating coastal areas where both detail and moderate
range are required.

Long pulse lengths (L - Offshore tiers) carry more energy, allowing for greater detection range,
crucial for identifying distant targets in open waters. However, longer pulses inherently reduce
range resolution, meaning closely spaced targets at long range might appear as a single,
elongated echo.
The ability to vary pulses by adjusting their length is a fundamental aspect of optimizing radar
performance for different scenarios. A shorter pulse provides better discrimination between
targets at close range, while a longer pulse increases the total energy transmitted, enhancing
detection of weaker targets at greater distances. For this research, understanding these pulse
characteristics is vital for developing effective HDR and decluttering techniques, as the optimal
pulse for capturing information in one zone (e.g., harbor) may be entirely different from another
(e.g., offshore).

Part 3b: Furuno Data Augmentation

The Furuno SDK and platform offer a comprehensive suite of software features designed to
enhance radar performance and operator interpretation. Among these, RezBoost (Effective
Azimuth Beam Width) is prominently touted for its ability to provide 'clearer' data, ostensibly
making it easier for an operator to discern real objects from clutter on the display. This function
claims to significantly reduce the effective horizontal beam width from a physical 3.8 degrees
down to approximately 1.9 degrees. The visual impact of RezBoost is compellingly
demonstrated in Figure 12, where the radar image processed with RezBoost (left panel) exhibits
sharper, more defined returns, appearing less spread out than the 'Standard' setting (right panel).
This sharpening is achieved by sophisticated digital signal processing that simulates the effect of
a narrower physical antenna beam. While this undoubtedly benefits human operators by reducing
angular spreading and improving the visual separation of closely spaced targets, its utility for the
computational research undertaken here is likely limited. There is a concern that RezBoost may
operate by applying internal filters or transformations that could potentially discard or smooth
out valuable raw data, particularly the subtle variations critical for high dynamic range (HDR)
extraction or the nuanced features required by machine learning models like the Masked Auto
Encoder (MAE). For this research, maintaining access to the purest, least-processed data is ideal.

Figure 12: RezBoost Max(3) vs Standard Settings

Beyond visual enhancements, the Furuno system also employs intelligent data handling, particularly
concerning interpolation and its interaction with the Dual Pulse functionality. Despite claims of being an
8-bit system, raw data collection consistently reveals only 17 distinct amplitude values, effectively
rendering it a 4-bit system with one additional level. This limited quantization presents a notable
challenge for extracting high dynamic range. However, when interpolation is activated, the system
provides a full 8-bit output while concurrently removing the Dual Pulse functionality and providing
additional radials. This suggests the presence of an internal algorithm generating synthetic or interpolated
data points to create a smoother transition in results and to fill in the perceived missing dynamic range.

Part 3c: ARPA vs mARPA & Furuno Implementation
Radar systems for collision avoidance are broadly categorized into ARPA (Automatic Radar Plotting Aid)
and mARPA (mini-ARPA), distinguished by their automation and target tracking capacity. Full ARPA
systems, typically found on larger commercial vessels, automatically detect, acquire, and track targets,
calculating critical collision parameters (CPA, TCPA) and providing comprehensive target data (ID,
bearing, range, COG, SOG). These systems require precise heading and speed inputs, powerful
processing (e.g., Kalman filtering), and a high-resolution radar front-end. In contrast, mARPA, common

on smaller recreational craft, requires manual target selection, tracks fewer targets (10-30), has slower
updates, lacks automatic hazard detection, and may occasionally drop tracks. While still valuable,
mARPA's limitations make it a less sophisticated collision avoidance tool. A more complete summary is
seen in Table 3.
The Furuno DRS4D-NXT radar, used in this project, possesses full ARPA capabilities, a significant
feature for a leisure-class solid-state Doppler radar. It automatically tracks up to 100 targets, providing
rapid speed and course vectors and detecting low-RCS objects. The ability of the Furuno NXT to generate
comprehensive tracking data, even when internal processing is bypassed for raw data capture, provides a
rich dataset for supervised training. The presence of full ARPA may also serve as a benchmark for
evaluating the performance of the algorithms developed within this study. Currently, there has been no
success in implementing ARPA/mARPA across any of the Furuno & Simrad radars in this project.

Part 4: Decluttering and Dynamic Range Algorithm Analysis
In maritime radar imaging, two of the most formidable challenges are (1) clutter and speckle noise, which
obscure real targets against a highly variable sea return, and (2) the wide dynamic range of reflectivities,
from faint buoys and small craft to large steel hulls, that must be rendered simultaneously. Effective
decluttering enhances detection and tracking performance, while extended dynamic-range processing
ensures that both low- and high-intensity echoes are visible in a single composite.

Figure 14: Overview of the three primary algorithms tested

To address these challenges, three broad classes of algorithms were evaluated, as shown in Figure 14.
Single‐Frame Noise‐Reduction Models operate on each radar sweep independently, estimating local
statistics such as mean, variance and patch similarity to distinguish noise from signal. Methods like Lee
filtering, wavelet denoising and BM3D adaptively smooth homogeneous regions while preserving edges.
Because they require no temporal alignment, these approaches offer the lowest latency and simplest
integration, but can only exploit spatial structure and typically blur small or low‐contrast targets.
Multi‐Frame Noise‐Reduction Models leverage a short sequence of consecutive sweeps, exploiting the
fact that true clutter (sea surface, stationary objects) remains correlated over time while speckle and
random noise do not. Techniques ranging from pixel‐wise median averaging and robust principal
component analysis (RPCA) to non‐local means across frames achieve significantly stronger noise
suppression, but at the expense of sensitivity to moving targets and the need for robust registration.
Multi‐Exposure (HDR) Fusion Models combine radar returns captured at different gain or exposure
settings, which encode complementary information: high‐gain sweeps reveal faint echoes but saturate
strong targets, while low‐gain sweeps preserve bright returns but lose weak signals. HDR fusion
algorithms (for example, exposure‐fusion or the MergeMertens method) automatically select and blend
the best‐exposed regions from each image, producing a single radar map that simultaneously displays
both ends of the intensity spectrum. This approach excels at extending dynamic range without explicit
sensor calibration, though it requires careful alignment to avoid ghosting and can blend in noise if the
exposures are poorly matched.

Part 5: Data Storage and Formatting + Hardware Updates
Part 5a: Storage & Conversion
NetCDF is a self-describing, array-oriented standard with built-in named dimensions, rich metadata and
chunked I/O. Any researcher can open a NetCDF file in Python, MATLAB, R or C and immediately
understand its contents; units, long names, provenance and all; without separate schema files or custom
parsers. In contrast, raw binaries (.bin), NumPy arrays (.npy) or MATLAB files (.mat) lack a universal
metadata convention; flat text (.csv/.txt) requires sidecar descriptors; and folder-wide compressors (e.g.
ZSTD) force full-file decompression even for small slices. The combination of portability, partial-read
performance and community conventions (such as CF-metadata) establishes NetCDF as the de facto
choice for long-term scientific data stewardship.
Raw radar sweeps arrive as CSV files; each row recording range, gain, angle and the time of the first
sweep. Immediately, these CSVs are ingested into a two-level NetCDF structure via a three-stage Rust
pipeline that performs deduplication, sanitization and conversion into a compressed Echo variable. Frontloading NetCDF ensures that every downstream tool (MATLAB’s ncread, Python’s netCDF4/xarray, R’s
ncdf4) reads the same self-describing, chunk-friendly archive, eliminating bespoke CSV parsers and
accelerating both interactive analysis and automated workflows.

Figure 19: High-Level overview of NetCDF data storage. Global data contains location and radar settings.

At the top level(Figure 19), each NetCDF file opens with a set of global attributes, radar latitude and
longitude, transmitter gain, start-of-sweep timestamp, and so on, followed by an unlimited “time”
dimension. Defining time as an unlimited axis makes it possible to append future sweeps without
rewriting the file. For every index along that axis, the two-dimensional Echo(gates, angle) variable is
stored and compressed in place using DEFLATE-9. Because all metadata lives in the header, (i.e. units,
long names, provenance) tools like MATLAB’s ncread, Python’s netCDF4/xarray or R’s ncdf4 can open
the file and immediately interpret every field without sidecar schemas or manual parsing.

Figure 20: Lower-level look at NetCDF structure. Scaled at 1:100

Drilling down (Figure 20), the Echo array sits on two dimensions; time (unlimited length) and angle (0–
8192 steps). The NetCDF4’s DEFLATE 9 filter (with byte-shuffle) was used to greatly reduce storage
size. Crucially, NetCDF’s chunked I/O lets readers fetch a single time-slice or angular subset without
decompressing the entire archive.

Figure 21: RUST processing pipeline for radar data. Converts CSV to NetCDF

To create the NetCDF4 files from massive datasets (>0.2 TB), a highly-parallelized Rust program (Figure
21) spins up a work queue and three stages of workers:
1. Deduplication, where a coordinator thread lists new CSVs and dispatches them to cleaners that drop
exact-duplicate records.

2. Sanitization, in which validators normalize data types, enforce 0 ≤ angle<360° (8192), and
quarantine any corrupt files.
3. Conversion, where threads batch-read cleaned CSV rows into memory structs and append them
directly into the NetCDF file, then write out the global attributes all at once.
Running all stages in parallel across multiple cores real run-time low; even though compression is CPUheavy, the work is amortized over each batch and scales nearly linearly with core count.
Once the canonical NetCDF is complete, simple Python or MATLAB scripts slice each time-step into 16bit PNG frames and assemble an MP4 via FFmpeg. By standardizing on NetCDF, we eliminated dozens
of bespoke CSV parsers, gained self-describing metadata, random-access subsetting, and cross-language
interoperability, all outweighing the raw speed/size gains of a one-off ZSTD archive.

Part 5c: Collection Software Improvements
Bringing the boot time down from several minutes to under twenty seconds began with swapping out the
bloated Raspberry Pi OS image for a leaner build (while still retaining GUI support). By stripping
unnecessary services and drivers, the Pi now reaches a useable prompt in under 20 s, compared with four
to six minutes on the previous install.
Simultaneously, the data‐capture code was refactored and ported from C into modern C++. The main
program shrank from roughly 2,000 lines to just over 500, achieved by removing redundant function
calls, consolidating I/O routines, and leveraging STL containers and RAII for resource management. The
result is a far more modular codebase, where the boost web server, radar‐SDK worker, calibration
handler, sweep sequencer, and file‐writer threads each live in their own component.
Figure 24 illustrates this multithreaded workflow: after initialization, three core modules spin up in
parallel, while dedicated threads manage range/gain sequencing and raw‐data/PNG writes. Pushing so
many concurrent threads, five in the main pipeline plus any callback threads from the radar SDK, has
begun to bump against the Pi 5’s four‐thread architectural limit, leading to scheduler contention under
peak load, resulting in severe data loss when adding additional processing threads (i.e. edge processing).
Future work will explore rebalancing responsibilities to avoid thread starvation or implementing more
‘offline’ processing.

Part 5d: Calibration Sequencing
Calibration proceeds in two tiers. As seen in Figure 25, the mini‐calibration varies five discrete ranges
(0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75 NM) and 100 gain settings (0–99), capturing five back‐to‐back
sweeps at each gain (discarding the first as a warm-up). With sea- and rain-clutter disabled, this produces
2,500 valid sweeps in one run. In high-RPM mode, a mini‐calibration completes in about 40 minutes; in
low-RPM it takes roughly 80 minutes.
For thorough system characterization, the full-calibration adds two interpolation modes and four
RezBoost settings (2×4 additional configurations), multiplying the sweep count to 20,000. At high RPM

this requires ~320 minutes (≈5 h 20 m), and at low RPM ~640 minutes (≈10 h 40 m). The resulting data
feed into automated routines that compute gain-vs-range response curves, quantify ADC linearity, and
generate lookup tables for real-time gain scheduling on the radar web server.

Part 6: Self-Supervised Temporal Exposure MAE Prototype
Part 6a: The Idea
Raw dual-pulse radar sweeps are reshaped into a four-dimensional tensor, range × azimuth ×
time/gain × pulse, and fed into a Masked Autoencoder that learns both motion and exposure
priors without any manual labels. In the first stage, 75 % of the voxels in each current frame are
randomly masked and reconstructed conditioned on the previous time slice; this forces the
encoder to internalize object trajectories and temporal consistency (see Figure 26and Figure 27).

Figure 26: Complete proposed MAE detection pipeline.

The second stage applies an exposure-aware mask, borrowing from SelfHDR, to treat clipped or
saturated low-gain voxels as missing data, compelling the network to fuse in high-gain returns
where needed. Combined, these self-supervised tasks teach the model to handle both dynamicrange extremes and moving targets on low-bit, non-Doppler radar hardware.

Figure 27: Visual representation of the 4D Radar Data Tensor the MAE is trained on

Part 6b: Results
With just 132 frames for training, 40 epochs of training at batch size 1, and an L₂+SSIM
reconstruction loss, the Temporal MAE captured some broad background structure and slow
object motion but struggles to resolve most targets. Reconstruction MSE remained high and
SSIM on masked regions produced blurred or incomplete restorations. The exposure-aware pass
improved contrast recovery in saturated areas but introduced edge artifacts, indicating that a
learnable or adaptive masking threshold may be required.
Part 7 Future
Part 7a: Algorithms and Model Development
The first priority will be to finalize the optimal S1/S2 gain values so that the radar can operate in
full HDR mode, ensuring maximal dynamic-range capture across all scenarios. Once the gain
window is locked in, work will shift to completing the preliminary HDR-to-object-detection
pipeline, whereby high-, medium- and low-gain frames are fused and fed directly into a
lightweight detection network. In parallel, the Masked Autoencoder prototype will undergo
further testing in both its temporal and exposure-aware configurations, leveraging a larger and

more varied training corpus plus adaptive masking schedules to improve reconstruction fidelity
and increase detection accuracy.

Part 7b: Experiments and Testing
The calibration web-server needs to be enhanced to support a truly end-to-end mini- and fullcalibration run; in addition to range and gain selectors, users will need controls for STC, pulse
length, RezBoost, rotation speed and interpolation, and the underlying threading model must be
refactored to eliminate dropped sweeps. Once the server is robust, system-level field trials will
validate both one-click calibration and HDR-detection performance under varied weather
conditions. If capacity allows, undergraduate-led efforts will integrate IMU/GPS data into radar
headers for precise georeferencing, add a synchronized RGB camera for multimodal objectlabelled datasets, and build a simple touch-accessible frontend so operators can deploy the entire
capture, calibration and HDR pipeline from a single panel.
