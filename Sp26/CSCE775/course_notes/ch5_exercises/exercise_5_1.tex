\section*{How Blackjack Works (Quick Primer)}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

If you have never played blackjack, here is all you need to know for this exercise.

\textbf{The goal.} Get a hand of cards whose values add up as close to 21 as possible, \textit{without going over}. If your total exceeds 21 you ``bust'' and lose immediately.

\textbf{Card values.} Number cards (2--10) are worth their face value. Face cards (Jack, Queen, King) are each worth 10. An Ace is special: it can count as either 1 or 11, whichever helps you more.

\textbf{A round of play.} Both you (the player) and the dealer start with two cards. You can see one of the dealer's cards (the ``showing'' card) but not the other. On your turn you have two choices:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Hit} --- take another card (adds to your total, but risks going over 21).
    \item \textbf{Stick} (also called ``stand'') --- stop taking cards and keep your current total.
\end{itemize}
After you stick, the dealer plays by a fixed rule: keep hitting until reaching 17 or higher, then stick. Whoever is closer to 21 without busting wins.

\textbf{Rewards.} Win $= +1$, Lose $= -1$, Draw $= 0$. There is no discounting ($\gamma = 1$), so the reward from the end of the game \textit{is} the return for every state visited during that game.

\textbf{What is a ``usable ace''?} If you hold an Ace that you are currently counting as 11 (and your total is still $\leq 21$), that Ace is called \textit{usable}. It acts like a safety net: if a future card would push you over 21, you can ``downgrade'' the Ace from 11 to 1 and avoid busting. Once downgraded, the Ace is no longer usable.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section*{The Policy Being Evaluated}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The textbook evaluates a very simple (and not very good) policy:
\begin{quote}
    \textit{If your hand totals 20 or 21, stick. Otherwise, hit.}
\end{quote}
This means the player keeps requesting cards until reaching 20 or 21 --- risky, because every extra card could push the total over 21.

\textbf{What the 3D plots show.} Each surface plots the \textit{value function} $V(s)$: how good, on average, is it to be in a particular situation? The axes are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Player sum} (12--21) --- your current card total.
    \item \textbf{Dealer showing} (Ace through 10) --- the one dealer card you can see.
    \item \textbf{Height} ($-1$ to $+1$) --- the average outcome from that situation. Positive means you tend to win; negative means you tend to lose.
\end{itemize}
There are two separate surfaces: one for hands \textit{with} a usable ace, one \textit{without}.

\section*{Answer 1: The big jump at player sums 20 and 21}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{What is happening in the plot.} The surface is mostly flat and negative (around $-0.6$ to $-0.8$) for player sums 12 through 19, then it suddenly shoots up to positive territory at sums 20 and 21.

\textbf{Why.} This jump happens because the policy \textit{changes its behavior} right at sum 20. Think of it in two contrasting scenarios.

\textit{Player sum is 19 or below --- the policy says ``hit.''}  The player is forced to take another card. With a sum of 19, almost any card except an Ace or a 2 will push the total over 21 (a bust). Specifically, only 3 out of every 13 cards are safe; the other 10 cause an instant loss. So the average outcome from sum 19 is very bad (about $-0.75$).

\textit{Player sum is 20 --- the policy says ``stick.''}  Now the player stops and keeps their 20. To beat a 20, the dealer must end up with exactly 21. That is hard to do. So the player wins most of these games, and the average outcome jumps to roughly $+0.15$ to $+0.45$ depending on what the dealer is showing.

The table below shows this cliff from the simulation (no usable ace case):

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        Player Sum & Avg.\ outcome vs.\ Dealer Ace & Avg.\ outcome vs.\ Dealer 10 \\
        \midrule
        19 & $-0.779$ & $-0.746$ \\
        20 & $+0.148$ & $+0.436$ \\
        21 & $+0.640$ & $+0.888$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{The jump from sum 19 to sum 20 is nearly a full point --- from almost-always-losing to usually-winning --- because the policy switches from hit to stick.}
\end{table}

\textbf{In short:} sums 20 and 21 are the only ones where the player actually \textit{stops and keeps a strong hand}. Everything below 20 is dragged down by the constant risk of busting while hitting.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section*{Answer 2: The drop when the dealer shows an Ace}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{What is happening in the plot.} Along the left edge of each surface (where the dealer's showing card is an Ace), the values are noticeably lower than everywhere else.

\textbf{Why.} An Ace is the best possible card for the dealer, for an intuitive reason: \textit{flexibility}.

Imagine the dealer flips over a 6 as their hidden card, giving them Ace + 6. They can count the Ace as 11 for a total of 17 and stick (a decent hand). Now imagine instead the hidden card is a 7, giving Ace + 7 = 18. Even better. And if a later hit would push the total over 21, the dealer just ``downgrades'' the Ace from 11 to 1 and keeps going --- the Ace acts like a built-in safety net for the dealer too.

Because of this flexibility, the dealer almost never busts when starting with an Ace. The bust rate for a dealer showing an Ace is only about 11.5\%. Compare that to a dealer showing a 5 or 6, where the bust rate is roughly 40\%. When the dealer busts, the player wins automatically --- so a low dealer bust rate means fewer free wins for the player.

\textbf{In short:} the dealer's Ace makes the dealer's hand very hard to beat, pulling the player's average outcome down across \textit{all} player sums.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section*{Answer 3: Usable ace helps at low sums}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{What is happening in the plot.} At the front of the surfaces (low player sums like 12, 13, 14), the \textit{usable ace} plot sits noticeably higher than the \textit{no usable ace} plot.

\textbf{Why.} Remember, under this policy the player hits on everything below 20. At low sums the player will have to hit \textit{multiple times}, and each hit risks a bust. Having a usable ace is like wearing a seatbelt during this process.

Here is a concrete example. Suppose the player's cards are Ace + 4, totaling 15 (counting the Ace as 11). The player hits and draws a 9. Normally $15 + 9 = 24$, which is a bust. But because the Ace is usable, it downgrades from 11 to 1, changing the total from 24 to 14. The player survives and gets to keep hitting.

Now consider the same situation \textit{without} a usable ace: the player has, say, 8 + 7 = 15. They hit and draw a 9. The total is $15 + 9 = 24$ --- bust, instant loss, $-1$ reward. No safety net.

This difference shows up clearly in the numbers. At player sum 12 against a dealer showing 10:
\begin{align}
    V(\text{with usable ace}) &= -0.279 \\[2pt]
    V(\text{without usable ace}) &= -0.567
\end{align}
With a usable ace the player still loses on average (negative value), but \textit{much} less often, because the ace absorbs hits that would otherwise cause a bust.

As the player sum gets closer to 20, this advantage shrinks --- there is less room for the ace to help, and at sums 20--21 the player sticks immediately regardless, so the usable ace barely matters.

\textbf{In short:} a usable ace is a ``get out of bust free'' card. It matters most at low sums where the player has to hit many times, making those states significantly less bad.

    {\large First-Visit vs.\ Every-Visit Monte Carlo}
\end{center}

\section*{Problem Statement}

Suppose every-visit MC was used instead of first-visit MC on the blackjack task. Would you expect the results to be very different? Why or why not?

\section*{Background: What is the difference?}

To answer this, we first need to understand the two flavors of Monte Carlo prediction.

\textbf{First-visit MC.} When computing the value of a state $s$, we look at each simulated game (episode). If the player passed through state $s$ during that game, we record the outcome --- but only for the \textit{first time} $s$ appeared in that episode. If somehow the player landed in state $s$ again later in the same game, we would ignore that second visit.

\textbf{Every-visit MC.} Same idea, except we count \textit{every} time $s$ appears in the episode, not just the first time. If $s$ shows up three times in one game, we record the outcome three times and average all of them together.

\textbf{When would this matter?} The two methods only differ when the \textit{same state is visited more than once within a single episode}. If every state only ever appears once per episode, the two methods are identical --- there is nothing to ``skip'' under first-visit, because there are no repeat visits.

\section*{Answer: No, the results would not differ at all}

In blackjack under this policy, \textbf{no state is ever visited twice in the same game}. The reason has to do with how the state is defined and how the game works.

Recall that a state is a triple: (player sum, dealer showing, usable ace). Consider what happens each time the player takes a card (hits):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The \textbf{dealer's showing card} never changes during a game --- it is dealt once and stays face-up. So this part of the state is fixed.
    \item The player's \textbf{usable ace} status can change, but only in one direction: a usable ace can be ``spent'' (downgraded from 11 to 1), but a spent ace cannot become usable again.
    \item The \textbf{player sum} changes with every hit. And here is the key: each card drawn has a value of at least 1, so the player sum \textit{strictly increases} with every hit --- unless the usable ace gets downgraded, in which case the sum drops by 10, but the usable-ace flag also flips. Either way, the overall state triple changes.
\end{itemize}

More concretely, think of it step by step. Suppose the player has sum 14 with no usable ace against a dealer showing 7 --- state $(14, 7, \text{no})$. They hit and draw a 5, moving to sum 19 --- state $(19, 7, \text{no})$. They hit again and draw a 3, moving to sum 22 --- bust (game over). At no point did the player return to state $(14, 7, \text{no})$, because the sum can only go up.

The only tricky case is with a usable ace: suppose the player has sum 15 with a usable ace --- state $(15, 7, \text{yes})$. They hit and draw a 9, pushing the total to 24. The ace downgrades ($-10$), bringing them to sum 14 --- but now the state is $(14, 7, \text{no})$. The usable-ace flag is different, so this is a \textit{different state} from before.

Since no state ever repeats within an episode, every visit \textit{is} the first visit. The two methods produce \textbf{exactly identical results}.

\section*{Empirical Verification}

To confirm this reasoning, we ran both first-visit and every-visit MC on the same 500{,}000 blackjack episodes. The difference between the two methods was verified to be exactly zero for all 200 states.

Some key statistics from the simulation:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Total state visits recorded (first-visit): 777{,}567
    \item Total state visits recorded (every-visit): 777{,}567 (identical)
    \item Episodes with any state revisited: 0 out of 500{,}000 (0\%)
    \item Maximum absolute difference across all states: 0.0000
\end{itemize}

\section*{When \textit{would} they differ?}

For completeness, the two methods \textit{would} produce different results in environments where the agent can revisit the same state within an episode. For example:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item A robot navigating a gridworld that can walk in circles (visiting the same cell multiple times before reaching a goal).
    \item Any episodic task where the state can ``reset'' or cycle back to a previous value.
\end{itemize}

Even in those cases, both methods converge to the true value function given enough episodes --- they just converge at slightly different rates. First-visit MC produces unbiased estimates. Every-visit MC is biased (slightly) but has lower variance, and the bias disappears as the number of episodes grows.

In blackjack, none of this matters because the two methods see exactly the same data.

\section*{Problem Statement}

What is the backup diagram for Monte Carlo estimation of $q_\pi$?

\section*{Background: What is a backup diagram?}

A \textit{backup diagram} is a small picture that shows how an algorithm computes (or ``backs up'') a value estimate. Think of it as a visual summary of the question: ``What information does the algorithm use to update its estimate for one state (or state-action pair)?''

The diagrams use two shapes:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Open circle} $\circ$ --- represents a \textit{state}. This is a situation the agent finds itself in (e.g., ``player sum is 15, dealer shows 7, no usable ace'').
    \item \textbf{Filled circle} $\bullet$ --- represents a \textit{state-action pair}. This is a situation \textit{plus} a specific action (e.g., ``player sum is 15, dealer shows 7, no usable ace, \textit{and the player hits}'').
    \item \textbf{Filled square} --- represents the \textit{terminal state} (end of the episode, e.g., the game is over).
\end{itemize}

The node at the \textit{top} of the diagram is the thing whose value we are estimating. Lines going downward show the information used to compute that estimate.

\section*{Answer}

The backup diagram for Monte Carlo estimation of $q_\pi$ is diagram~(c) above. It looks almost identical to the MC backup for $v_\pi$, with one key difference.

\textbf{The difference: what is at the top.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item MC for $v_\pi$ starts at an \textbf{open circle} (a state $s$). We are asking: ``How good is it to \textit{be in} this state?''
    \item MC for $q_\pi$ starts at a \textbf{filled circle} (a state-action pair $s, a$). We are asking: ``How good is it to \textit{be in} this state \textit{and take} this specific action?''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Everything below the top is the same.} After the starting action is taken, the environment produces a reward and a next state, the policy picks the next action, and so on --- a single sampled path all the way down to the terminal state (the filled square at the bottom). The return from that entire trajectory is used to update the estimate of $q_\pi(s, a)$.

\section*{Why does it look this way?}

The shape of a backup diagram reflects two design choices:

\textbf{1. How wide is it?} (Does it branch, or is it a single path?)
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{DP diagrams are wide} --- they fan out to consider \textit{every} possible action and \textit{every} possible next state. This is possible because DP has access to the full model (the transition probabilities $p(s', r \mid s, a)$).
    \item \textit{MC diagrams are narrow} --- they follow just \textit{one} sampled trajectory. MC does not need a model; it just plays out one episode and sees what happens.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. How deep is it?} (Does it go one step or to the end?)
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{DP diagrams are shallow} --- they look ahead exactly one step and then plug in an estimated value for the next state (bootstrapping).
    \item \textit{MC diagrams are deep} --- they go all the way to the end of the episode. MC does \textit{not} bootstrap; it waits for the actual outcome.
\end{itemize}

For $q_\pi$ specifically, the diagram starts one level lower than $v_\pi$, because we have already committed to a particular action. The first transition out of the top node is the environment's response to that action (producing a reward and a next state), not the policy choosing which action to take.

    {\large Incremental Monte Carlo ES}
\end{center}

\section*{Problem Statement}

The pseudocode for Monte Carlo ES is inefficient because, for each state-action pair, it maintains a list of all returns and repeatedly calculates their mean. It would be more efficient to use techniques similar to those explained in Section~2.4 to maintain just the mean and a count (for each state-action pair) and update them incrementally. Describe how the pseudocode would be altered to achieve this.

\section*{Background: Why is the original version inefficient?}

The Monte Carlo ES algorithm on page~99 of the textbook stores data like this:

\begin{quote}
    $Returns(s, a) \leftarrow$ an ever-growing list of numbers
\end{quote}

Every time the agent finishes a game and visits state-action pair $(s, a)$, the algorithm appends the return $G$ to the list $Returns(s, a)$ and then recomputes the average of the \textit{entire} list. As the agent plays more and more games, these lists get longer and longer. After 500{,}000 games, a frequently visited pair might have a list with tens of thousands of entries, and the algorithm recomputes the average over all of them every single time.

This is wasteful in two ways:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Memory:} We are storing every return we have ever seen. For 200 state-action pairs visited thousands of times each, that is a lot of numbers sitting in memory.
    \item \textbf{Computation:} Recomputing the average of $n$ numbers takes $O(n)$ time. As $n$ grows, this gets slower and slower. But the new average only differs from the old average by a tiny adjustment --- surely we can compute it faster.
\end{itemize}

\section*{The trick from Section 2.4: Incremental mean update}

Section~2.4 of the textbook shows that you do not need to store the entire list. You only need two things: the \textit{current average} and a \textit{count} of how many values you have seen so far.

The key formula (Equation~2.3 in the textbook) is:
\begin{align}
    Q_{n+1} = Q_n + \frac{1}{n}\Big[R_n - Q_n\Big]
\end{align}
In plain English: to update the average after seeing a new value, take the old average $Q_n$, compute the ``error'' (how far the new value $R_n$ is from the old average), and nudge the average toward the new value by a fraction $1/n$.

This is an instance of the general update pattern that appears throughout reinforcement learning:
\begin{align}
    \text{NewEstimate} \leftarrow \text{OldEstimate} + \text{StepSize} \times \Big[\text{Target} - \text{OldEstimate}\Big]
\end{align}

The step size $1/n$ gets smaller as we see more data, which makes sense: early observations should move the average a lot, but after thousands of observations, each new one should barely budge it.

\section*{Answer: The modified pseudocode}

We replace the $Returns(s, a)$ lists with two simpler data structures: $Q(s, a)$ (the running average) and $N(s, a)$ (the count). Here is the full modified algorithm:

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.92\textwidth}{
\textbf{Monte Carlo ES (Incremental), for estimating $\pi \approx \pi_*$}
\vspace{4pt}

\textbf{Initialize:}\\[2pt]
\hspace*{1em}$\pi(s) \in \mathcal{A}(s)$ (arbitrarily), for all $s \in \mathcal{S}$\\
\hspace*{1em}$Q(s, a) \in \mathbb{R}$ (arbitrarily), for all $s \in \mathcal{S}$, $a \in \mathcal{A}(s)$\\
\hspace*{1em}$N(s, a) \leftarrow 0$, for all $s \in \mathcal{S}$, $a \in \mathcal{A}(s)$
\hfill {\color{garnet}$\triangleleft$ \scriptsize replaces $Returns(s,a)$ lists}

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Loop forever} (for each episode):\\[2pt]
\hspace*{1em}Choose $S_0 \in \mathcal{S}$, $A_0 \in \mathcal{A}(S_0)$ randomly (exploring starts)\\
\hspace*{1em}Generate an episode from $S_0, A_0$, following $\pi$:\\
\hspace*{2em}$S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, A_1, R_2, \ldots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T$\\
\hspace*{1em}$G \leftarrow 0$\\
\hspace*{1em}\textbf{Loop} for each step of episode, $t = T{-}1, T{-}2, \ldots, 0$:\\[2pt]
\hspace*{2em}$G \leftarrow \gamma G + R_{t+1}$\\
\hspace*{2em}Unless the pair $S_t, A_t$ appears in $S_0, A_0, S_1, A_1, \ldots, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}$:\\[2pt]
\hspace*{3em}$N(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow N(S_t, A_t) + 1$
\hfill {\color{garnet}$\triangleleft$ \scriptsize increment count}\\
\hspace*{3em}$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \dfrac{1}{N(S_t, A_t)}\Big[G - Q(S_t, A_t)\Big]$
\hfill {\color{garnet}$\triangleleft$ \scriptsize incremental update}\\[4pt]
\hspace*{3em}$\pi(S_t) \leftarrow \arg\max_a\, Q(S_t, a)$
}}
\end{center}

\section*{What changed, step by step}

Three things changed from the original pseudocode:

\textbf{1. Initialization.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{Removed:} $Returns(s, a) \leftarrow$ empty list, for all $s \in \mathcal{S}$, $a \in \mathcal{A}(s)$
    \item \textit{Added:} $N(s, a) \leftarrow 0$, for all $s \in \mathcal{S}$, $a \in \mathcal{A}(s)$
    \item $Q(s, a)$ was already initialized (arbitrarily). No change needed there.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. Inside the loop (when a first-visit state-action pair is found).}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{Removed:} ``Append $G$ to $Returns(S_t, A_t)$''
    \item \textit{Removed:} ``$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow \text{average}(Returns(S_t, A_t))$''
    \item \textit{Added:} ``$N(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow N(S_t, A_t) + 1$''
    \item \textit{Added:} ``$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \frac{1}{N(S_t, A_t)}\big[G - Q(S_t, A_t)\big]$''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Everything else stays the same.} The episode generation, the return calculation ($G \leftarrow \gamma G + R_{t+1}$), the first-visit check, and the policy improvement step ($\pi(S_t) \leftarrow \arg\max_a Q(S_t, a)$) are all unchanged.

\section*{Why this works}

The incremental formula produces \textit{exactly} the same running average as recomputing from the full list every time. Here is the intuition with a small example.

Suppose we have seen three returns for a particular state-action pair: 5, 3, and 7.

\textit{List-based approach:}
\begin{align}
    Q &= \text{average}(\{5, 3, 7\}) = \frac{5 + 3 + 7}{3} = 5.0
\end{align}

\textit{Incremental approach:}
\begin{align}
    N &= 1,\quad Q = 0 + \tfrac{1}{1}[5 - 0] = 5.0 \\[2pt]
    N &= 2,\quad Q = 5.0 + \tfrac{1}{2}[3 - 5.0] = 5.0 + (-1.0) = 4.0 \\[2pt]
    N &= 3,\quad Q = 4.0 + \tfrac{1}{3}[7 - 4.0] = 4.0 + 1.0 = 5.0
\end{align}
Same result, but we never stored the list $\{5, 3, 7\}$. We only kept track of $Q$ and $N$.

\section*{Complexity comparison}

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
        & \textbf{Original (list-based)} & \textbf{Incremental} \\
        \midrule
        Memory per $(s,a)$ pair & $O(n)$ --- grows forever & $O(1)$ --- just $Q$ and $N$ \\
        Time per update & $O(n)$ --- re-averages list & $O(1)$ --- one multiply, one add \\
        Total memory & Unbounded & $2|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|$ (fixed) \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{The incremental version uses constant memory and constant time per update, regardless of how many episodes have been run.}
\end{table}

\section*{Summary}

Replace the ever-growing $Returns(s, a)$ lists with a simple counter $N(s, a)$. Instead of appending $G$ to a list and recomputing the average, increment the counter and nudge the current estimate $Q(s, a)$ toward $G$ using the step size $1/N(s, a)$. This produces identical results with $O(1)$ memory and $O(1)$ computation per update instead of $O(n)$ for both.

\begin{center}
    {\Large\color{garnet}\textbf{Exercise 5.5}}\\[6pt]
    {\large First-Visit vs.\ Every-Visit Estimators in a Simple MDP}
\end{center}

\section*{Problem Statement}

Consider an MDP with a single nonterminal state and a single action that transitions back to the nonterminal state with probability $p$ and transitions to the terminal state with probability $1 - p$. Let the reward be $+1$ on all transitions, and let $\gamma = 0.9$. Suppose you observe one episode that lasts 10 steps. What are the first-visit and every-visit estimators of the value of the nonterminal state?

\section*{Background: Setting up the problem}

This exercise strips everything down to the simplest possible MDP --- just one nonterminal state, one action, and one coin flip that decides whether the episode continues or ends. It is designed to highlight a subtle difference between first-visit and every-visit Monte Carlo.

\textbf{The MDP.} There is only one nonterminal state, call it $s$. From $s$, the agent takes its only action and one of two things happens:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item With probability $p$: the agent transitions \textit{back to $s$} and receives reward $+1$.
    \item With probability $1 - p$: the agent transitions to the \textit{terminal state} and receives reward $+1$.
\end{itemize}

Every transition gives $+1$ regardless, and $\gamma = 0.9$ (future rewards are discounted). Unlike the $\gamma = 1$ case, the return from an episode is \textit{not} simply the number of steps --- each successive reward is multiplied by another factor of $\gamma$.

\textbf{The true value.} For reference, the true value of state $s$ satisfies the Bellman equation:
\begin{align}
    v_\pi(s) &= 1 + \gamma \, p \cdot v_\pi(s) + \gamma (1-p) \cdot 0
\end{align}
The first term is the immediate reward ($+1$). With probability $p$ the agent loops back to $s$, receiving the discounted future value $\gamma \, v_\pi(s)$. With probability $1 - p$ the agent terminates (the terminal state has value 0). Solving:
\begin{align}
    v_\pi(s) &= \frac{1}{1 - \gamma p}
\end{align}
For example, if $p = 0.9$ and $\gamma = 0.9$, then $v_\pi(s) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.81} = \frac{1}{0.19} \approx 5.263$.

\textbf{The observed episode.} We see exactly one episode that lasted 10 steps. The episode looks like this:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    state/.style={circle, draw=black90, thick, minimum size=20pt, inner sep=0pt, font=\small},
    terminal/.style={rectangle, fill=black90, minimum size=12pt, inner sep=0pt},
    every node/.style={font=\small},
    >=stealth
]
    \node[state] (s0) at (0,0) {$s$};
    \node[state] (s1) at (1.6,0) {$s$};
    \node[state] (s2) at (3.2,0) {$s$};
    \node at (4.6,0) {$\cdots$};
    \node[state] (s9) at (6.0,0) {$s$};
    \node[terminal] (T) at (7.6,0) {};

    \draw[->, thick] (s0) -- node[above]{\scriptsize $+1$} (s1);
    \draw[->, thick] (s1) -- node[above]{\scriptsize $+1$} (s2);
    \draw[->, thick] (s2) -- node[above]{\scriptsize $+1$} (4.1,0);
    \draw[->, thick] (5.1,0) -- node[above]{\scriptsize $+1$} (s9);
    \draw[->, thick] (s9) -- node[above]{\scriptsize $+1$} (T);

    \node[below=4pt of s0] {\scriptsize $t=0$};
    \node[below=4pt of s1] {\scriptsize $t=1$};
    \node[below=4pt of s2] {\scriptsize $t=2$};
    \node[below=4pt of s9] {\scriptsize $t=9$};
    \node[below=4pt of T] {\scriptsize terminal};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

The agent visits state $s$ at time steps $t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9$ (ten visits total), then terminates after the tenth transition. Because $\gamma = 0.9$, each reward is worth less the further into the future it occurs.

\section*{Answer: First-visit MC estimator}

\textbf{What first-visit MC does.} It looks at the episode and finds the \textit{first} time state $s$ was visited. That happens at time step $t = 0$. It computes the \textit{discounted} return from that point onward:
\begin{align}
    G_0 &= R_1 + \gamma R_2 + \gamma^2 R_3 + \cdots + \gamma^9 R_{10} \notag \\[2pt]
    &= 1 + 0.9 + 0.9^2 + \cdots + 0.9^9 \notag \\[2pt]
    &= \sum_{k=0}^{9} 0.9^k = \frac{1 - 0.9^{10}}{1 - 0.9} = \frac{1 - 0.3487}{0.1} \approx 6.5132
\end{align}

All subsequent visits to $s$ (at $t = 1, 2, \ldots, 9$) are \textit{ignored} because first-visit MC only counts the first occurrence per episode.

Since we observed only one episode, the first-visit estimate is just that single return:
\begin{align}
    \boxed{V_{\text{first-visit}}(s) \approx 6.513}
\end{align}

\section*{Answer: Every-visit MC estimator}

\textbf{What every-visit MC does.} It looks at \textit{every} time state $s$ was visited in the episode and records the discounted return from each visit. State $s$ was visited 10 times, at $t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9$. The return from each visit is the discounted sum of rewards from that point to the end:

\begin{align}
    G_t &= \sum_{k=0}^{9-t} \gamma^k = \frac{1 - \gamma^{10-t}}{1 - \gamma}
\end{align}

Computing each one with $\gamma = 0.9$:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Visit at time $t$} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{$G_t$} \\
        \midrule
        $t = 0$ & $(1 - 0.9^{10})/0.1$ & $6.5132$ \\
        $t = 1$ & $(1 - 0.9^{9})/0.1$ & $6.1258$ \\
        $t = 2$ & $(1 - 0.9^{8})/0.1$ & $5.6953$ \\
        $t = 3$ & $(1 - 0.9^{7})/0.1$ & $5.2170$ \\
        $t = 4$ & $(1 - 0.9^{6})/0.1$ & $4.6856$ \\
        $t = 5$ & $(1 - 0.9^{5})/0.1$ & $4.0951$ \\
        $t = 6$ & $(1 - 0.9^{4})/0.1$ & $3.4390$ \\
        $t = 7$ & $(1 - 0.9^{3})/0.1$ & $2.7100$ \\
        $t = 8$ & $(1 - 0.9^{2})/0.1$ & $1.9000$ \\
        $t = 9$ & $(1 - 0.9^{1})/0.1$ & $1.0000$ \\
        \midrule
        & \textbf{Sum} & $\mathbf{41.381}$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Notice how each later visit sees a smaller discounted return --- not only are there fewer rewards left, but the discounting further shrinks their contribution. Compare the first visit ($G_0 = 6.513$) to the last visit ($G_9 = 1.0$).

The every-visit estimator averages all 10 returns:
\begin{align}
    V_{\text{every-visit}}(s) &= \frac{G_0 + G_1 + \cdots + G_9}{10} = \frac{41.381}{10}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
    \boxed{V_{\text{every-visit}}(s) \approx 4.138}
\end{align}

\section*{Deriving the sum in closed form}

The sum of all returns can be written compactly. Each return is $G_t = \frac{1 - \gamma^{10-t}}{1-\gamma}$. Summing over all 10 visits:
\begin{align}
    \sum_{t=0}^{9} G_t &= \frac{1}{1-\gamma} \sum_{t=0}^{9} \bigl(1 - \gamma^{10-t}\bigr) = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\Biggl[10 - \sum_{n=1}^{10} \gamma^n\Biggr] \notag \\[4pt]
    &= \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\Biggl[10 - \gamma\,\frac{1 - \gamma^{10}}{1 - \gamma}\Biggr]
\end{align}
Plugging in $\gamma = 0.9$:
\begin{align}
    &= \frac{1}{0.1}\Biggl[10 - 0.9 \times \frac{1 - 0.9^{10}}{0.1}\Biggr] = 10\Bigl[10 - 0.9 \times 6.5132\Bigr] = 10\bigl[10 - 5.8619\bigr] = 41.381
\end{align}
So $V_{\text{every-visit}} = 41.381 / 10 = 4.138$, confirming our table.

\section*{Why are the two estimates so different?}

The first-visit estimate is $6.513$ and the every-visit estimate is $4.138$ --- a significant gap. Here is the intuition.

\textbf{First-visit MC} only uses the return from $t = 0$, which captures the \textit{full discounted episode} (all 10 rewards, progressively discounted). This is a single sample of $v_\pi(s)$.

\textbf{Every-visit MC} averages the returns from \textit{all} visits, including late ones that only see a fraction of the episode. The visit at $t = 9$ only has one remaining reward ($G_9 = 1.0$), while the visit at $t = 5$ sees five rewards but discounted ($G_5 = 4.095$). These later visits systematically produce \textit{lower} returns --- not because the state is worse, but because there is less episode remaining \textit{and} each reward is further discounted.

This is the source of every-visit MC's \textit{bias}. The later visits within the same episode do not provide independent samples of $v_\pi(s)$. They are ``leftovers'' from the same trajectory, biased downward because the remaining discounted return is shorter. Averaging them in pulls the estimate well below the first-visit value.

\textbf{The effect of discounting.} With $\gamma = 0.9$, the gap between the two estimators is actually \textit{larger} in relative terms than with $\gamma = 1$. Discounting causes the later-visit returns to shrink faster (compare $G_9 = 1.0$ here vs.\ $G_9 = 1$ with $\gamma = 1$ --- both happen to be 1 in this case, but the first-visit return drops from 10 to 6.513), so the average gets pulled down more aggressively.

\section*{A general formula}

For this particular MDP with discount factor $\gamma$, if an episode lasts $n$ steps, the two estimators are:
\begin{align}
    V_{\text{first-visit}} &= \frac{1 - \gamma^{n}}{1 - \gamma} \\[6pt]
    V_{\text{every-visit}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\frac{1 - \gamma^{n-t}}{1 - \gamma} = \frac{1}{n(1-\gamma)}\Biggl[n - \gamma\,\frac{1 - \gamma^{n}}{1 - \gamma}\Biggr]
\end{align}

For our case ($n = 10$, $\gamma = 0.9$), these give $V_{\text{FV}} \approx 6.513$ and $V_{\text{EV}} \approx 4.138$. With more episodes, the bias of every-visit MC would shrink as the estimates converge, but from a single episode the difference is striking.

\section*{Comparison with $\gamma = 1$}

It is instructive to see how the answers change when discounting is removed. With $\gamma = 1$, all rewards have equal weight:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}
        \toprule
        & $\boldsymbol{\gamma = 1}$ & $\boldsymbol{\gamma = 0.9}$ & \textbf{Effect of discounting} \\
        \midrule
        First-visit & $10$ & $6.513$ & Lower (future rewards worth less) \\
        Every-visit & $5.5$ & $4.138$ & Lower (same effect, compounded) \\
        Ratio (FV / EV) & $1.82$ & $1.57$ & --- \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

In both cases, the every-visit estimate is substantially lower than the first-visit estimate due to the bias from averaging partial-episode returns. Discounting reduces both estimates but affects the first-visit return more in absolute terms (drops from 10 to 6.5), since it discounts 10 full terms.

\begin{center}
    {\Large\color{garnet}\textbf{Exercise 5.6}}\\[6pt]
    {\large Weighted Importance Sampling for Action Values}
\end{center}

\section*{Problem Statement}

What is the equation analogous to (5.6) for \textit{action} values $Q(s, a)$ instead of state values $V(s)$, again given returns generated using behavior policy $b$?

\section*{Background: What is Equation (5.6)?}

Before writing the action-value version, let us recall the state-value version. Equation (5.6) from the textbook is the \textit{weighted importance sampling} estimator for $V(s)$:
\begin{align}
    V(s) \;\doteq\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)}\; \rho_{t:T(t)-1}\; G_t}{\displaystyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)}\; \rho_{t:T(t)-1}} \label{eq:wis_v}
\end{align}
where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{T}(s)$ is the set of all time steps where state $s$ was visited (across all episodes).
    \item $T(t)$ is the termination time of the episode containing time step $t$.
    \item $G_t$ is the return (discounted sum of rewards) from time $t$ onward.
    \item $\rho_{t:T(t)-1}$ is the \textit{importance-sampling ratio} from time $t$ to the end of the episode:
    \begin{align}
        \rho_{t:T(t)-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T(t)-1} \frac{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}{b(A_k \mid S_k)}
    \end{align}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What does this equation do?} We are trying to estimate the value of state $s$ under the \textit{target} policy $\pi$, but all our data was generated by a different \textit{behavior} policy $b$. The importance-sampling ratio $\rho$ corrects for this mismatch. It reweights each observed return by how much more (or less) likely that trajectory was under $\pi$ compared to $b$. The ``weighted'' version divides by the sum of the weights rather than by the number of samples, which produces lower variance than the ordinary version (Eq.~5.5).

\section*{The key insight: what changes for $Q(s, a)$?}

When estimating $V(s)$, we condition on being in state $s$ at time $t$. The importance-sampling ratio must correct for \textit{every action from $t$ onward}, including the action at time $t$ itself, because the target and behavior policies might choose different actions.

When estimating $Q(s, a)$, we condition on being in state $s$ \textit{and} having already taken action $a$ at time $t$. The action at time $t$ is already fixed --- it is $a$, and both policies ``agree'' on it (it is the action that was observed). So we do \textbf{not} need to correct for the action at time $t$. The importance-sampling ratio only needs to cover the actions from time $t + 1$ onward.

Think of it this way:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item For $V(s)$: ``I'm in state $s$. What if I had followed $\pi$ from here?'' --- must correct the action \textit{at} $t$ and all future actions.
    \item For $Q(s, a)$: ``I'm in state $s$ and I took action $a$. What if I had followed $\pi$ \textit{after} this?'' --- only need to correct future actions (from $t+1$).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Answer}

The weighted importance sampling estimator for $Q(s, a)$ is:
\begin{align}
    \boxed{Q(s, a) \;\doteq\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s,a)}\; \rho_{t+1:T(t)-1}\; G_t}{\displaystyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s,a)}\; \rho_{t+1:T(t)-1}}}
\end{align}
where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{T}(s, a)$ is the set of all time steps where state $s$ was visited \textit{and} action $a$ was taken.
    \item $G_t$ is the return from time $t$ onward (same as before).
    \item $\rho_{t+1:T(t)-1}$ is the importance-sampling ratio starting from $t+1$ (one step later than before):
    \begin{align}
        \rho_{t+1:T(t)-1} = \prod_{k=t+1}^{T(t)-1} \frac{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}{b(A_k \mid S_k)}
    \end{align}
\end{itemize}

If $t+1 > T(t) - 1$ (i.e., the episode ended immediately after taking action $a$), then the product is empty and $\rho_{t+1:T(t)-1} = 1$ by convention. This makes sense: if there are no future actions to correct for, no reweighting is needed.

\section*{Side-by-side comparison}

The two equations differ in exactly two places:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
        & \textbf{State values $V(s)$} (Eq.~5.6) & \textbf{Action values $Q(s,a)$} \\
        \midrule
        Sum over & $t \in \mathcal{T}(s)$ & $t \in \mathcal{T}(s, a)$ \\
        \addlinespace
        IS ratio & $\rho_{t:T(t)-1}$ & $\rho_{t+1:T(t)-1}$ \\
        \addlinespace
        Ratio starts at & action at time $t$ & action at time $t+1$ \\
        \addlinespace
        Corrects for & all actions from $t$ & all actions \textit{after} $t$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section*{A concrete example}

Suppose we are estimating $Q(s, \text{hit})$ in blackjack, using data from a random behavior policy $b$ (50/50 hit or stick). The target policy $\pi$ is stick-on-20-or-21.

Imagine an episode where at time $t$ the agent is in state $s$ and takes action ``hit.'' After that, the episode continues for two more steps before terminating:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item At time $t$: state $s$, action $a = \text{hit}$ (this is the pair we are estimating)
    \item At time $t+1$: some state $s_1$, action $a_1 = \text{hit}$
    \item At time $t+2$: some state $s_2$, action $a_2 = \text{stick}$, then terminal
\end{itemize}

For $V(s)$, the IS ratio would cover all three actions:
\begin{align}
    \rho_{t:t+2} &= \frac{\pi(\text{hit} \mid s)}{b(\text{hit} \mid s)} \cdot \frac{\pi(\text{hit} \mid s_1)}{b(\text{hit} \mid s_1)} \cdot \frac{\pi(\text{stick} \mid s_2)}{b(\text{stick} \mid s_2)}
\end{align}

For $Q(s, \text{hit})$, we skip the first factor (since we already know action ``hit'' was taken):
\begin{align}
    \rho_{t+1:t+2} &= \frac{\pi(\text{hit} \mid s_1)}{b(\text{hit} \mid s_1)} \cdot \frac{\pi(\text{stick} \mid s_2)}{b(\text{stick} \mid s_2)}
\end{align}

Dropping that first factor reduces the variance of the ratio, because there is one fewer random term in the product. This is one reason why off-policy methods for action values can sometimes be more stable than for state values --- the importance-sampling ratios are shorter.

\section*{Summary}

The weighted importance sampling equation for $Q(s, a)$ is identical to Equation (5.6) for $V(s)$ except: (1) we sum over time steps where the specific pair $(s, a)$ was visited, and (2) the importance-sampling ratio starts at $t + 1$ instead of $t$, because the action at time $t$ is already determined to be $a$ and does not need correction. This shorter ratio means less variance per sample.

\section*{Problem Statement}

In learning curves such as those shown in Figure~5.3, error generally decreases with training, as indeed happened for the ordinary importance sampling method. But for the weighted importance sampling method, error first \textit{increased} and then decreased. Why do you think this happened?

\section*{Background: What Figure 5.3 shows}

Figure~5.3 (Example~5.4) plots the mean squared error (MSE) of two off-policy estimators as a function of the number of episodes, for a single blackjack state. The behavior policy is random (50/50 hit or stick), and the target policy is stick-on-20-or-21. The true value of the state is approximately $-0.27726$.

The two curves behave very differently:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Ordinary importance sampling:} starts with high error and decreases monotonically.
    \item \textbf{Weighted importance sampling:} starts with \textit{low} error, then the error \textit{increases} for a while, before eventually decreasing toward zero.
\end{itemize}

The question is: why does the weighted method get \textit{worse} before getting better?

\section*{Answer: The initial estimate is accidentally good}

The explanation comes from understanding what weighted importance sampling computes after just one or a few episodes.

\textbf{Step 1: What happens after the very first episode.}

Recall the weighted importance sampling formula:
\begin{align}
    V(s) = \frac{\sum_t \rho_t \, G_t}{\sum_t \rho_t}
\end{align}

After observing just one episode that visits state $s$, the estimate is:
\begin{align}
    V(s) = \frac{\rho_1 \, G_1}{\rho_1} = G_1
\end{align}

The importance-sampling ratio \textit{cancels out entirely}. The estimate is simply the observed return $G_1$ from that episode, regardless of how unlikely the trajectory was under the target policy. This is a key property of weighted IS: with a single sample, it always returns the observed return itself.

\textbf{Step 2: Why is this ``accidentally'' close to the true value?}

The behavior policy generates episodes by playing blackjack randomly. The return from any given episode is either $+1$ (win), $-1$ (lose), or $0$ (draw). After one episode, the estimate is one of these three values. The true value is $-0.27726$, which is between $-1$ and $0$.

By luck of averaging, the very first few estimates tend to land near the true value. If the first episode is a loss ($G = -1$), the estimate is $-1$, which is only $0.72$ away from $-0.27726$. If it is a win ($G = +1$), the estimate is $+1$, which is $1.28$ away. Averaged over 100 runs (as in the figure), the initial MSE turns out to be small because the weighted IS estimate is just the raw return --- a reasonable number in the right ballpark.

\textbf{Step 3: Why does the error then \textit{increase}?}

As more episodes are observed, the importance-sampling ratios $\rho_t$ stop canceling. Some episodes have large ratios (trajectories that are much more likely under the target policy than the behavior policy), and some have small ratios (or zero, if the behavior policy took an action the target policy would never take).

The weighted average now starts mixing together returns with very different weights. During this intermediate phase, the estimate can be dominated by a few episodes with large ratios, pulling it away from the true value. The estimator is \textit{biased} --- specifically, it is biased toward the value under the behavior policy $v_b(s)$ rather than $v_\pi(s)$.

In the early episodes:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The estimate starts near $G_1$ (which happened to be close to $v_\pi(s)$ on average).
    \item As more data arrives, the bias toward $v_b(s)$ kicks in. Since $v_b(s) \neq v_\pi(s)$ (the random policy evaluates the state differently than the stick-on-20-or-21 policy), this bias temporarily \textit{increases} the error.
    \item The estimate is being pulled from its accidentally-good starting point toward the wrong value ($v_b$), before eventually accumulating enough weighted samples to converge toward the correct value ($v_\pi$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 4: Why does the error eventually decrease?}

With enough episodes, the weighted IS estimator converges to the true value $v_\pi(s)$. The bias shrinks to zero as the denominator $\sum \rho_t$ grows, and the estimate settles in. The weighted estimator's low variance then pays off, and the MSE drops below that of ordinary IS.

\section*{A simple analogy}

Imagine guessing someone's age. Your first guess (50) happens to be close to the truth (48). But then someone tells you ``most people in this room are 30'' (the behavior-policy bias). You adjust your estimate toward 30, and for a while your estimate is \textit{worse} than your initial lucky guess. Eventually, with more information, you correct back toward 48. That temporary dip away from the truth is exactly what happens to the weighted IS estimator.

\section*{Why doesn't ordinary IS show this pattern?}

Ordinary importance sampling divides by the \textit{number of samples} $n$, not by the sum of the weights. After one episode:
\begin{align}
    V_{\text{ordinary}}(s) = \frac{\rho_1 \, G_1}{1} = \rho_1 \, G_1
\end{align}

The ratio $\rho_1$ does \textit{not} cancel. If the trajectory happened to be 10 times more likely under $\pi$ than $b$, the estimate is $10 \times G_1$, which can be wildly off. So the ordinary IS estimator starts with \textit{high} error (due to variance from extreme ratios) and gradually improves as the average of many $\rho_t G_t$ values stabilizes. There is no ``accidentally good'' starting point to lose.

\section*{Summary}

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.14\textwidth} p{0.37\textwidth} p{0.37\textwidth}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Phase} & \textbf{Ordinary IS} & \textbf{Weighted IS} \\
        \midrule
        Early & High error: extreme $\rho$ values amplify returns, causing wild estimates & Low error: $\rho$ cancels in the ratio, estimate $\approx$ raw return (accidentally close to truth) \\
        \addlinespace
        Middle & Error decreasing: averaging tames the variance & Error increasing: bias toward $v_b(s)$ pulls estimate away from $v_\pi(s)$ \\
        \addlinespace
        Late & Converges (unbiased, high variance) & Converges (bias vanishes, low variance) \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The weighted IS estimator's error hump is the cost of its bias: it starts lucky, drifts toward the behavior-policy value, then finally corrects to the true target-policy value. The ordinary IS estimator pays upfront with high variance but improves monotonically.
