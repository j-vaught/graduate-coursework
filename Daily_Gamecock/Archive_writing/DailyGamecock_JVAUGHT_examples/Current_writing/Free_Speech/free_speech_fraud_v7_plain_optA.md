# Column: Free speech has become "My Speech" at USC

On September 18, 2024, Milo Yiannopoulos and Proud Boys founder Gavin McInnes spoke to a sparse audience behind metal barricades at the University of South Carolina's Russell House. Uncensored America — a supposedly "nonpartisan, nonprofit organization dedicated to fighting for freedom of speech" — had its $3,576.99 funding request rejected by the Student Senate. Conservative media called it censorship, and FIRE agreed, calling it "a clear violation of the First Amendment."

They claim to be the victims. But the movement behind that stage has its own record to answer for. An organization cannot demand that universities platform speakers while running databases to intimidate professors into silence. That is not a free speech position. That is a *my* speech position.

According to FIRE's own Scholars Under Fire database, over 75 percent of incidents targeting scholars for their speech now come from the political right — not the left. The same movement that screams censorship when a student senate votes against funding its event is the one doing the censoring.

Turning Point USA claims to champion free speech — while maintaining a Professor Watchlist, a publicly searchable database targeting over 300 academics. Charlie Kirk said to CBS News: "Our site is not intolerance or censorship. It's about information... gives parents and students a way to decide if they need to look deeper and learn more."

PEN America saw it differently, calling the Watchlist "a noxious purveyor of precisely what it claims to deride: the intimidation and ostracization of those who express controversial views on campus."

At Diablo Valley College, political science professor Albert Ponce had a lecture on white supremacy edited into a two-minute clip and circulated through Fox News and Breitbart. He received threats against himself and his nine-year-old daughter. Family photos were posted online. He told his daughter not to open the mail.

Boston University sociology professor Saida Grundy, listed for a tweet, said: "It was the greatest stress of my life, and I literally was just coming off having stage-three breast cancer. It was far more stressful than the cancer, far more stressful."

David S. Cohen, a Drexel University law professor listed for pro-choice scholarship, said: "He's being touted as this paragon of free speech who just wanted to engage with people who disagree with him. In fact, he was behind a website that targeted those who he disagreed with and broadcast information about us to his extremist readers with the clear intention that they should take some kind of action against us."

At Arizona State, TPUSA employees physically assaulted a professor. At Ohio State, Treva B. Lindsey received a surge of messages with "racist language and threats of sexual violence." Free speech for them. Silence for their critics.

Consider who the movement chose as its ambassadors to USC. Yiannopoulos's controversies include appearing to defend pedophilia and provoking riots at UC Berkeley that caused $100,000 in damages. McInnes said "violence doesn't feel good, justified violence feels great, and fighting solves everything."

McInnes relies on the First Amendment when he preaches that "sunlight is the best disinfectant." Yet when the Southern Poverty Law Center labeled the Proud Boys a hate group, he filed a defamation lawsuit to silence them. The flagship speakers of the free speech movement sue their critics when criticized.

Since 2021, the movement's allies have enacted more than 70 bills and policies across 26 states restricting what professors can teach, which books students can read, and which perspectives universities can value in hiring. In 2025 alone, 21 censorship bills became law in 15 states — the highest single-year total ever recorded. Every one of those legislatures was Republican-controlled. The American Library Association reports that 72 percent of censorship demands come from organized conservative movements, elected officials, and school board members.

In 2021, Gov. Henry McMaster signed the REACH Act, mandating what aspects of American history universities must teach. The South Carolina Commission on Higher Education then requested section-specific syllabuses from USC, making it easy to identify exactly what individual professors were teaching.

Kirk Randazzo, chair of USC's political science department, said: "My greater concern is that by simply monitoring things, the state is potentially creating a chilling effect that might influence or impact how faculty teach material." Carol Harrison, a USC history professor, said: "The chill is already here."

An open letter from South Carolina's Republican Congressional delegation called on university presidents to "eradicate" any teaching of critical race theory — and identified a USC professor by name. The 2025-2026 session brought HB 3184 banning DEI in admissions and employment, SB 368 prohibiting DEI offices statewide, and the FORUM Act designating campus outdoor areas as public forums. Free speech for the demonstrator. Government control over the classroom.

FIRE's own faculty survey paints the picture on the ground. Fifty-five percent of conservative faculty say they hide their political views to protect their jobs. Thirty-five percent of all faculty have toned down their writing to avoid controversy — compared to just 9 percent during the McCarthy era. The chill is real. But it is not coming from the direction they claim.

So how did USC respond to all of this? After the Student Senate denied Uncensored America's funding, the administration reversed it under legal pressure. That same night, over 1,000 students gathered at Blatt Field for "Blatt Bonanza" — free food, bouncy houses, a rage room, and selfies with the university president. Yiannopoulos spoke to empty chairs at Russell House. Bouncy houses are not a free speech policy. They are an admission that the university knows exactly what it is platforming and would rather distract than confront.

The deeper question is why the university funds these organizations at all. Student activity fees subsidize events featuring men with ties to hate groups. USC adopted the Chicago Principles in June 2023 and rocketed from 246th to 22nd in FIRE's rankings. But a high ranking and a principled institution are not the same thing.

If the cost of defunding organizations like Uncensored America is also losing funding for progressive groups, that trade might be worth making. Losing student-fee-funded liberal advocacy is a smaller price than continuing to hand platforms to people whose primary contribution to public discourse is making a professor's nine-year-old daughter afraid to open the mail.

Yes, progressive students are hypocrites too. At USC, 27,000 people signed a petition to cancel the McInnes-Yiannopoulos event. Many of the same voices later decried the arrest of pro-Palestinian demonstrators as suppression of protected expression. But the progressive contradiction involves petitions and protests. The conservative contradiction involves legislation, state power, and entire fields of study banned by law.

The next time Turning Point USA or Uncensored America invokes the First Amendment at USC, ask one question. Would you apply this principle to the professors being fired in Texas and Florida for teaching concepts you oppose?

If they would, they should say so — loudly, publicly, and to the state legislators who fund their movement. They should condemn the Professor Watchlist and fight HB 3184 and SB 368 with the same energy they brought to a $3,577 funding request for a roast.

If they wouldn't, they should stop pretending. What they call free speech is *my* speech — and the loudest voices for it on this campus belong to a movement doing more to destroy it than any student petition or bouncy house ever could.
