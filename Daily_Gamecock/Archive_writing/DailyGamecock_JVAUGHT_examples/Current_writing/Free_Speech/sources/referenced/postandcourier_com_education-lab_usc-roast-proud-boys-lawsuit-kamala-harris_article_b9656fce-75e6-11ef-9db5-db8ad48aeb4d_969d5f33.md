 COLUMBIA — A University of South Carolina student group’s “roast” of Vice President Kamala Harris was attended by a rowdy crowd and drew around two dozen protesters who objected to appearances of Gavin McInnes, founder of the far-right Proud Boys group, and far-right British commentator Milo Yiannopoulos.

Inside the university’s Russell House student union building, a crowd filling the ballroom watched McInnes throw out slurs, repeat unfounded claims about Harris’ racial identity, make sexist attacks on her political rise and get into arguments with audience members, some of whom stormed out of the room.

During his part of the talk, Yiannopoulos read out a list of names of USC faculty and students who he claimed he wanted to sue, following Uncensored America’s own claim earlier in the day that it would sue the university.

South Carolina State Troopers stand outside the Russell House on the University of South Carolina campus before a “roast” event organized by Uncensored America, Wednesday, Sept. 18, 2024, in Columbia.

 
 
 
 
 
 Henry Taylor/Staff
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
The event, organized by self-described free-speech student group Uncensored America, and titled using a vulgar misspelling of Harris’ name, had drawn widespread criticism and media attention.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Protesters outside the student union said they didn’t think the university should have allowed the event, pointing to the Proud Boys’ ties to political violence. Protestors wanted to show that many on campus are opposed to the speakers’ ideas.

“We don’t want it in our campus and in our city,” said Iris Griffin, a 20-year-old junior.

Over 27,000 people had signed a petition calling on the university to cancel or modify the "roast," and USC student organizations, state lawmakers and the NAACP criticized the event and the university's decision to allow it, pointing to the speakers' history of bigoted comments and its vulgar theming.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

University President Michael Amiridis and board of trustees Chairman Thad Westbrook denounced "hate and bigotry" and "vile and juvenile rhetoric" in an August statement while reasserting that the school would defend constitutionally-protected speech.

McInnes, a co-founder of Vice Media, started the Proud Boys in 2016, but formally left it in 2018.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Some of the groups’ members later were sentenced to prison terms for participating in the Jan. 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol, and their organization was designated a hate group by the Southern Poverty Law Center.

Protesters and counter-protesters chat over a police barricade outside of the Russell House on the USC campus before a “roast” event organized by Uncensored America, Wednesday, Sept. 18, 2024, in Columbia.

 
 
 
 
 
 Henry Taylor/Staff
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
A self-described “western chauvinist,” McInnes once posted a video titled “Ten Things I Hate about Jews,” which was retitled “Ten Things I Hate About Israel" before the video was eventually deleted. 

During the event, he said he would have addressed "the whole Nazi thing,” referring to critics' characterizations, but that he assumed the audience didn’t believe it.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Once an editor at Breitbart News, Yiannopoulos resigned after appearing to defend sexual relations between men and boys in video clips, which he both apologized for and said were taken out of context, according to reporting by the Associated Press.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

In one act during the USC event, Yiannopoulos poured a prop bucket labeled as bodily fluid onto an aide. He later falsely claimed that the Jan. 6, 2021 U.S. Capital attack wasn't violent. 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
A USC student slides down an inflatable slide during the Blatt Bonanza event Sept. 18, 2024. The event was down the street from the university's student union, where Uncensored America held a controversial event at the same time.

 
 
 
 
 
 Sydney Dunlap/Special to The Post and Courier
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Before the roast, state lawmakers and activists spoke out against the event on the steps of the Statehouse, condemning what they described as hate speech and calling for the General Assembly to pass hate crimes legislation.

“If we sit silent and allow these guys to promote hate, hate will continue to grow,” state Rep. Leon Howard, D-Columbia, said.

A few minutes down the road on campus, alternative programming for students featured inflatables, yard games, crafts and a rage room. The line for the event stretched down the street 30 minutes before it began, and students were greeted with free t-shirts, a jazz band and a dinner of burgers and hot dogs. 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

By 8:30 p.m., a representative of the university said about 1,300 people had checked in.

During that event, Amiridis mingled and took photos with students. When asked about the roast event at the Russell House, he said the university did not endorse any activity.

"It was a group of students, a student organization, that requested the space, and they have every right to do so, and they decided to do it," Amirids said. "So we didn't have a choice. We would not start, you know, allowing one group and not allowing another to have the space that they need, as long as they follow procedures."

Hours before the event started, Uncensored America’s founder Sean Semanko claimed that the group would sue USC, citing First Amendment violations.

Semanko said the lawsuit would stem from a university Student Senate vote to deny funding for the event. He described that decision as "failing to remain viewpoint neutral."

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

As of the evening of Sept. 18, no filing under Uncensored America's name appeared in online federal or state court records. Semanko had a document resembling a legal filing at the press conference announcing the suit, but would not show it to reporters, citing advice from his lawyer.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

University spokesman Jeff Stensland said he could not comment on any pending litigation.

The Student Senate, part of the university's student government, voted 27-10 on Sept. 11 to deny around $3,576 in requested event funding to Uncensored America, according to a report from The Daily Gamecock, USC's campus newspaper.

During debate over the funding request, the senate's speaker Maura Grace Hamilton reportedly said that senate and university policy requires votes on funding allocations to be viewpoint neutral, and that a senate committee had already approved the request. Others argued that the event's vulgar marketing violated university policy.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

All of the Student Senate's procedures were followed during the Sept. 11 meeting, Hamilton said in a Sept. 18 statement to The Post and Courier, declining to comment further.

Another protest is planned for Sept. 19, when some students expect to march from Greene Street to the Statehouse to demonstrate against the university’s handling of the event, Courtney McClain, a USC alumna, Howard University student and NAACP activist, said at the Statehouse.

Sydney Dunlap contributed to this report.
