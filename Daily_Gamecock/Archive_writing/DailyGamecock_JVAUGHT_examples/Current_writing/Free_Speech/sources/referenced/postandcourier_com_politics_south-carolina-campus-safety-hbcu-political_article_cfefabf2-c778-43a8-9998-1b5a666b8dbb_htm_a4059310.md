 COLUMBIA — Campus safety officials at South Carolina’s major public universities met to discuss ways to bolster their internal security measures following several high-profile threats, ranging from bomb threats at historically Black colleges to a school shooting hoax.

And while the Sept. 16 meeting primarily centered around policies and safety protocols, the three-hour Statehouse hearing also touched on the possibility for political violence following the assassination of conservative commentator Charlie Kirk at a Utah university.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

The campus leaders — which included representatives from the University of South Carolina, Clemson, the College of Charleston, Coastal Carolina and South Carolina State — spoke of working within an increasingly complex threat environment where the lives, work and travel patterns of college students and faculty is a top concern.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

What that looks like can very widely. Campus police officers are regularly tasked with staffing events from the small and uncontentious to the sprawling and inflammatory, said USC Chief of Police Chris Wuchenich.

Though most of the more than 600 events worked by his staff in 2024 required only minimal security, six events featuring controversial figures, like Proud Boys founder Gavin McInnes, required substantially greater attention, he said.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Those more contentious speaking events, he said, can often invoke considerations like the gauging of admission requirements and a full-on walkthrough of the venue to potential collaborations with the State Law Enforcement Division.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

By the count, SLED officials say they have been called to assist on some 79 on-campus events this calendar year.

Others spoke to expanding the scope of their security perimeters to encompass multiple city blocks and differentiating concerns between hosting events involving controversial figures indoors versus outdoors. Others noted the sheer difficulty in promoting on-campus principles for free speech in a collegiate setting.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Chip Searson, chief of police and vice president of public safety at the College of Charleston, noted events often cannot be delayed until cooler heads prevail, and often take place at times where emotions are at their rawest.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

“Bad guys know no boundaries,” he told lawmakers. “They’re walking amongst us every single day.”

 

Each campus’ ability to respond to incidents varied widely.

South Carolina State Chief of Public Safety Richard Johnson spoke of a small staff of just 10 full-time law enforcement officers available for shift work. The limits mean campus leadership must sometimes turn to private security to augment officers’ occasional seven-days-per-week patrol schedule.

While the University of South Carolina boasts one of the state’s largest on-campus police forces, its leaders found itself struggling with disinformation online during a recent “swatting” incident at the start of the semester, and still lacks a specific accountability tool at the state level to prosecute the still-undetermined perpetrator of the hoax.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Most spoke of simple policy solutions to address their shortcomings, including legislation enabling law enforcement to go after out-of-state instigators of false threats of violence to implementing cellphone applications and artificial intelligence technology to faster identify and respond to threats.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Some lawmakers were surprised at a lack of training for students in reacting to potential threats on campus. One lawmaker, Pickens Republican Rep. Lee Gilreath, questioned Clemson’s campus safety officer on whether she believed the campus should revisit its policy prohibiting students and faculty from carrying firearms on school grounds.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

The official, Associate Vice President of Public Safety Kimberly Spears-McNatt, referred the question to campus leadership.

Legislators themselves alluded to their own concerns. Chairman Rep. Tim McInnis, R-Myrtle Beach, mentioned lawmakers had met for four hours that morning discussing their own safety protocols after Kirk’s Sept. 10 shooting death.

Speaking with reporters after the meeting, Beaufort Republican Rep. Shannon Erickson, who chairs the state’s powerful committee on Education and Public Works, said she was recently requested by the chamber’s sergeant-at-arms, Mitch Dorman, to travel with a police officer to a speaking engagement in her district as a precaution.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

“It's sad, but we're going to have to be smarter about where we do hold events,” she told reporters. “And I go places a lot of times by myself. Perhaps I shouldn't be doing that.”
