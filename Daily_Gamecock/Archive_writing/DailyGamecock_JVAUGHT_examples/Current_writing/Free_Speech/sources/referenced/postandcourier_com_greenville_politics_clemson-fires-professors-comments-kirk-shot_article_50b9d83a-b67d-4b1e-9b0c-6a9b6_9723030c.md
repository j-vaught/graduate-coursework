 CLEMSON — Clemson University fired two professors over their comments disparaging conservative activist and podcaster Charlie Kirk, who was shot to death Sept. 10 in Utah.

The announcement, at 9:12 a.m. Sept. 16 came less than 24 hours after a Clemson Board of Trustees meeting where members announced President James Clements was charged “to take immediate and appropriate measures regarding the employees in question.”

The firings come as Coastal Carolina University and the University of South Carolina Union disciplined employees who posted criticism of Kirk online in recent days.

The Clemson news was celebrated by the political right, but multiple experts told the newspaper the university — in its haste to quiet what had become a firestorm of criticism for not taking action against the employees — might have left itself open to a legal fight in state and federal courts.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Carol Harrison, president of South Carolina’s conference of the American Association of University Professors, said the dismissals also create a climate of fear that’s not good for education.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

“Firing teachers without due process, it intimidates faculty and it intimidates students, right?” Harrison said. “I think it touches on students’ freedom to learn as well as faculty freedom to teach, and it creates a kind of widespread fear, and that fear really does damage the learning environment.”

Administrators had already fired a Clemson staff member for similar reasons ahead of the trustee meeting.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

The university has not named any of the three dismissed employees.

In its statement, Clemson responded to concerns that it took too much time — about five days — to fire the employees. S.C. House Majority Leader Davey Hiott had said the day before that Clemson “faltered” on the issue, unlike a local public school district, and that the House Republican Caucus would ensure this is never repeated.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

“Every deliberation reflects the University’s unwavering commitment to conduct all actions in full compliance with institutional policies, state and federal laws, and the foundational principles of due process,” Clemson’s statement said.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Universities must follow procedures when dismissing professors, Harrison said.

“As a rule, all sides have a chance to address accusations,” she said.

That, she said, does not appear to have happened in this case.

Under Clemson’s faculty manual, a professor can be dismissed with cause if their actions "could reasonably be construed as having extremely adverse effects upon Clemson University." The manual also includes an appeals process.

If the professors do file a grievance, they will be given a chance to lay out their case before a committee of their peers in the Clemson Faculty Senate, who in turn can make a recommendation to the president. The professors can then appeal to the Board of Trustees.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

After that, they can sue in state or federal court, said attorney John Crangle, a government watchdog advocate based in Columbia.

Under the First Amendment, government entities cannot suppress the speech, ideas and expression of employees just because it doesn’t like them, said Allen Chaney, legal director of the American Civil Liberties Union of South Carolina.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 

Private companies can do that, but public employers such as Clemson must establish a “factual record” that a worker’s core duties have been affected by the speech, he said.

”It just can't be the case that that has happened here,” Chaney said. “It's not like these professors tried to go back into their classrooms and there were student walkouts and nobody was taking their classes and other professors were refusing to collaborate with them.”

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Any records of public condemnation for the employees’ social media posts that Clemson might produce were largely manufactured and amplified by other government officials — specifically, people in the conservative political establishment who are running for office in 2026, Chaney added.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Multiple Republican gubernatorial candidates — including U.S. Rep. Nancy Mace, U.S. Rep. Ralph Norman, Attorney General Alan Wilson, S.C. Sen. Josh Kimbrell and Lt. Gov. Pamela Evette — weighed in with reports of the employees’ remarks and calls for their termination.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

“It's not a naturally occurring phenomenon that a random audio technology assistant professor at Clemson posted something to his followers on Twitter that would create outrage,” Chaney said. “The outrage has been manufactured by the political establishment that it was seeking out these sorts of comments.”

Jack Lyle, hoarse from days of campaigning for the employees’ firing, said his organization — the Clemson College Republicans — is pleased with the president’s decision.

“Their speech was protected by the First Amendment,” Lyle said. “However, it did violate the code of ethics and a number of different agreements that they will have signed, or at the very least will have been subject to, being that they were employed by Clemson University.”

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

The university’s actions over the past week have not been embraced by all students.

“It’s really, really sad,” said Elysium Sigmond, a Clemson student and member of the campus YDSA. “I mean, professors getting fired for what they say?”

After trustees met, the Student Senate voted to object to the university’s decision this month to eliminate all identity-based commissions, which had advised administrators on issues around the LGBTQ community, veterans, Black, Asian and Latino students, disabled students and women.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Professors are being suspended or fired around the country because of their commentary around the Kirk assassination, said Mark Criley, senior program officer in the Department of Academic Freedom, Tenure, and Governance. He urged anyone in academia whose job is in jeopardy over political speech to contact the organization.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

“The state and federal government efforts to constrain faculty speech, faculty freedom in the classroom and faculty research are a serious crisis in higher education,” Criley said.

The Clemson firings also were not the only ones to hit South Carolina campuses this week.

An unidentified part-time instructor at the University of South Carolina Union was "relieved of his teaching duties," according USC spokesman Jeff Stensland, after what the he described as a "vulgar post that appeared online." 

The move came after Mace and state Rep. Luke Rankin, R-Laurens, posted pictures of an alleged Facebook comment mocking Rankin's efforts to get a Laurens County road named after Kirk. Rankin said online that he had spoken with USC Union leadership about the alleged remarks Sept. 15. 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

"I appreciate the swift response from the administration in addressing this matter and ensuring the integrity of the classroom environment is upheld," he wrote on social media.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Similarly, a Coastal Carolina University employee was fired Sept. 16 and another suspended without pay for social media posts they made and shared about Kirk.

Education Lab reporter Ian Grenier contributed to this story.
