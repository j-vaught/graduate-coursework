# Free Speech Lessons from Berkeley's Milo Yiannopoulos Incident

Based on analysis by law professors Erwin Chemerinsky and Howard Gillman, three core principles emerge from the UC Berkeley controversy:

## 1. Viewpoint Neutrality is Essential

The First Amendment protects offensive speech. University officials correctly rejected demands to ban Yiannopoulos based on his message's offensiveness, recognizing that "the government cannot prevent or punish speech based on the viewpoint expressed."

Universities have an obligation to remain neutral on the content of expression, even when that expression is controversial or deeply objectionable to significant portions of the campus community.

## 2. Institutions Must Prevent "Heckler's Veto"

Campuses bear responsibility for protecting speakers from disruption by hostile audiences. As the authors explain, allowing protesters to silence speakers through intimidation undermines free speech itself. Berkeley spent weeks arranging security with officers from nine UC campuses.

The heckler's veto—where a speaker is silenced because of opposition from audience members—represents a fundamental violation of free speech principles. Universities must invest resources in preventing this outcome.

## 3. Safety as a Last Resort

When violent disruption becomes genuinely uncontrollable, cancellation becomes justified—but only then. The authors note that approximately 150 masked agitators committed property damage and violence that campus security couldn't manage, making cancellation a necessity rather than censorship.

Cancellation of events should occur only when universities have exhausted reasonable security measures and face genuine safety threats.

## The Broader Takeaway

The incident demonstrates that protecting free speech doesn't mean consequences-free platforms. It means distinguishing between legitimate protest (protected) and violent disruption (punishable), while resisting pressure to suppress unpopular speakers solely because their ideas offend.

The Berkeley case illustrated both the importance of free speech protection and the real costs—financial, social, and institutional—of defending those principles consistently.
