# State Laws Restricting Classroom Discussions on Race and Gender

## Overview

More than a dozen states have enacted laws limiting classroom instruction on race and gender topics. According to PEN America, 2025 has been "a banner year for censorship at a state level."

## Key State Laws

**Alabama (SB 129, 2024)**
Restricts teaching "divisive concepts," including material suggesting any race is inherently superior or inferior. The law has prompted faculty to cancel projects and reconsider course offerings.

**Mississippi (HB 1193, 2025)**
Bans programs promoting diversity, equity, and inclusion. According to legal challenges, the law would prohibit discussions of slavery, civil rights movements, and LGBTQ+ rights.

**Texas (SB 37, 2025)**
Grants governing boards authority over general-education curricula and allows elimination of programs with low enrollments. This threatens ethnic studies and gender studies programs.

**Ohio (SB 1, 2025)**
Requires syllabi to be posted publicly and mandates investigation of complaints when students claim divergent opinions aren't accommodated. Universities have suspended majors including Africana studies and women and gender studies.

**Florida (2023 law)**
First state to restrict general-education courses teaching "identity politics" or systemic racism theories.

## Common Concerns

Critics argue these laws use vague language, chill academic freedom, and encourage self-censorship among faculty members. The cumulative effect creates an environment where professors worry about their employment status when teaching topics related to race, gender identity, and sexuality.

## Indirect Censorship

States have increasingly adopted "indirect" approaches to classroom censorship, using euphemistic language about "divisive concepts" and "intellectual diversity" rather than direct bans. This approach makes it more difficult to challenge the laws legally while still achieving the intended effect of constraining faculty speech.

## Faculty Response

The laws have prompted some universities to proactively eliminate courses and programs, and individual faculty members report increased anxiety about their classroom instruction and curricular decisions. Some have begun self-censoring to avoid potential complaints or institutional discipline.
