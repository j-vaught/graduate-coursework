# Free Speech Debate Intensifies After Controversial Hearing with University Presidents

**Author:** Kiara Alfonseca
**Published:** December 15, 2023

## Summary

When presidents of Harvard, University of Pennsylvania, and MIT testified before Congress about campus antisemitism in early December, the hearing revealed deep tensions around free speech protections in higher education.

Lawmakers pressed the university leaders on whether calls for "genocide against the Jewish people" violated campus conduct codes. The presidents responded that speech determinations require legal analysis and commitment to expression freedoms. This technical approach drew criticism from some who felt it lacked moral clarity.

UCLA law professor Eugene Volokh explained the nuance: "The truth is that advocacy of genocide is sometimes protected under the First Amendment and sometimes not."

University of Pennsylvania President Elizabeth Magill subsequently resigned following the backlash. Harvard President Claudine Gay later acknowledged she should have prioritized moral condemnation alongside legal explanation.

## Key Distinctions

### Protected Speech

The First Amendment shields offensive and hateful expression, even extreme cases like Nazi march permits (1977) and Westboro Baptist Church funeral picketing (2011).

### Unprotected Speech

Speech inciting imminent lawless action, threatening bodily harm, or causing immediate breaches of peace falls outside First Amendment protection.

Private universities must comply with Title VI nondiscrimination laws, while public universities face First Amendment constraints.

## Emerging Concerns

A 2022 Knight-Ipsos poll showed student confidence in campus free speech protections dropped from 73% to 47% since 2016. Reports indicate professor punishment attempts surged from four instances (2000) to 145 (2022).

Civil liberties advocates warn that expanded speech restrictions create dangerous precedents for political expression across the ideological spectrum.
