# Project 2025 and Higher Education: Key Proposals and Concerns

## Major Proposals

Project 2025, a conservative blueprint for a potential second Trump administration, would fundamentally transform higher education through several sweeping changes:

**Dismantling the Education Department**: The plan calls for breaking up the agency and dispersing its functions across other departments. The Office for Civil Rights would move to the Department of Justice, while the Treasury Department would handle federal student loans.

**Student Loan Overhaul**: The blueprint aims to end Biden's loan forgiveness programs, eliminate income-driven repayment options like SAVE, and privatize federal lending. It would also terminate Public Service Loan Forgiveness and create a new government corporation to manage remaining federal loans.

**Accreditation Reform**: Project 2025 proposes severing the connection between accreditation and federal student aid eligibility, allowing states to serve as their own accreditors and reducing what the plan characterizes as "ideological preferences" imposed by current agencies.

**Rolling Back Protections**: The plan would end all ongoing Title IX investigations and rescind recent protections for LGBTQ+ students and sexual assault survivors while stripping DEI requirements from federal regulations.

## Academic Freedom Implications

Critics express serious concerns about democratic governance. Michael Podhorzer of the Center for American Progress states the agenda "cannot be implemented by anything remotely like a democratic process." Maya Wiley warns the blueprint constitutes a "road map to ending" democracy itself.

The proposal to eliminate negotiated rulemaking—currently required for Title IX regulations—would reduce stakeholder input in policymaking, raising questions about institutional autonomy and faculty voice in governance decisions.

## Accreditation and Political Control

Allowing states to serve as their own accreditors represents a particularly significant threat. Governors who have built their political brand on attacking higher education—like Florida's Ron DeSantis—would be handed a powerful new mechanism to punish universities whose faculty or academic programs they disapprove of, and to reward universities that promote their ideological preferences.
