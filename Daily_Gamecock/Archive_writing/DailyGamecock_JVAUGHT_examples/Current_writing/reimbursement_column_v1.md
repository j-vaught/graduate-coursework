# The reimbursement system works — if you're allowed to see it

*J.C. Vaught · Opinion Column · The Daily Gamecock*

A student organization treasurer submits a reimbursement request. They followed the process: filed through Garnet Gate, got Senate Finance approval, paid out of pocket, uploaded receipts. Weeks pass. They call the Department of Student Life — the office that works with student organizations every day — and ask where the money is. The answer: "We don't have visibility into that." They call the finance office. Finance tells them to talk to Student Life. The student hangs up, opens their bank app, and stares at a balance that hasn't changed since October.

The system is working as designed. That's the problem.

---

Last April, I wrote in this paper that it was time to take politics out of student organization funding. The argument was straightforward: letting undergraduate senators with no financial training control hundreds of thousands of dollars in allocations produced inconsistency, favoritism, and delays. The university needed professional administration.

The university partially delivered. The Student Organization Funding Allocation Board — SOFAB — launched for the 2025-2026 cycle, shifting a chunk of funding decisions away from the purely legislative process and into a structure housed in the Leadership and Service Center. It was a real step. But the new system introduced a failure mode nobody warned students about: an interdepartmental visibility gap that makes reimbursement delays harder to diagnose, harder to escalate, and harder to fix than they were before.

---

Here is how the process works now. A student organization submits a funding request through Garnet Gate. The request routes through the appropriate branch — SOFAB or the Student Government treasurer's office — and goes through an LSC risk assessment. The Student Body Treasurer reviews it. Senate Finance votes. If approved, the organization spends the money out of pocket and submits a reimbursement to the Student Life finance team.

That last handoff is where requests vanish. The approval side of the pipeline — Garnet Gate, Senate Finance, the treasurer's office — is visible to Student Life staff. The reimbursement processing side is not. The finance software that tracks whether a reimbursement has been claimed, is being processed, or was rejected does not integrate with Garnet Gate. Student Life advisors who talk to organization leaders every week cannot look up reimbursement status. They have to send an email to the finance office and wait for a response, just like the students do.

This means the people who are supposed to help student organizations navigate university bureaucracy are themselves locked out of the bureaucracy. An advisor sitting across from a frustrated treasurer has no more information than the treasurer does. They cannot tell the student whether the request was received, whether a document is missing, whether someone in finance rejected it for a formatting error three weeks ago and never sent a notification. They are flying blind in the office that is supposed to be the control tower.

Student Body Treasurer Reese Lycan put the stakes plainly: "It's really hard for college students to be waiting on a $2,000 check for months at a time." Some organizations have been waiting since October — more than six months — for reimbursements on expenses the university already approved. These are not students trying to game a system. These are students who followed every step, spent their own money on faith that the institution would pay them back, and then watched the request disappear into a department they cannot contact and an office that cannot see it.

---

When no one in the student-facing department can see the reimbursement pipeline, no one in that department can be held accountable for delays. Finance processes requests on its own timeline. Student Life cannot escalate what it cannot see. The student is stuck between two offices pointing at each other across a wall that the university itself built.

This is not a personnel failure. I want to be clear about that, because the same point applied in April and it applies now. The staff in Student Life and in the finance office may be perfectly competent. But a system where the department responsible for student relationships has zero read access to the financial pipeline is broken by design. You do not need bad actors to produce bad outcomes. You just need a bad org chart.

Matt Hinds, Director of Administration in the Department of Student Life, has acknowledged that the majority of SEC peer institutions have alternative funding mechanisms — direct-pay models, procurement cards, unified dashboards — that reduce or eliminate the reimbursement bottleneck entirely. Some schools never ask students to front the money in the first place. South Carolina built a new system and kept the most painful part of the old one: the assumption that 19-year-olds can float thousands of dollars for months and simply wait.

The budget math makes it worse. The restructured funding pool split roughly $340,000 between Student Government ($185,000) and SOFAB ($155,000). Two smaller pools instead of one larger one, stricter interpretations of allowable expenses, and the same reimbursement bottleneck at the end. More organizations competing for less money, then waiting longer to be paid back for money the university already said yes to.

---

The fix is not complicated. It does not require a new office or a new budget line. It requires three things.

First, shared dashboard access. Student Life staff need read access to the reimbursement tracking system so they can answer student questions in real time instead of sending emails into the void. Second, status visibility for submitting organizations — something as simple as a package tracker that shows "received," "in review," "approved," "payment issued." Students track their DoorDash orders with more precision than they can track a $2,000 university reimbursement. Third, and most importantly, the university should move toward a direct-pay or procurement card model for approved allocations. If Senate Finance and SOFAB have already approved an expense, there is no reason the student should be the one swiping their debit card and hoping the university pays them back before rent is due.

A university that asks 18-year-olds to front thousands of dollars on approved expenses and then hides the repayment timeline behind an interdepartmental firewall is not running a funding system. It is running a loyalty test for students who happen to have savings accounts. Organizations led by students without financial cushions — first-generation students, students working multiple jobs, students without parental backup — are the ones this system punishes most. The reimbursement model is a wealth filter dressed up as a process.

Last spring, I argued that professional administration would fix student organization funding. The university took a step, and it deserves credit for that. But professionalism does not mean transferring opacity from student volunteers to staff offices. It means transparency: clear timelines, visible pipelines, and accountability that does not dead-end at a department boundary. If Student Life cannot see the reimbursement pipeline, students cannot either. That is not a system. That is a wall — and the students who followed every rule are the ones standing on the wrong side of it.
