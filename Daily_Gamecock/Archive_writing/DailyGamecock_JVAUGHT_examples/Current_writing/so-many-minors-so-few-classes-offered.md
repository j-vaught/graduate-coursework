# Column: USC Is Falsely Advertising Its Minors

**Author:** Legare Jenkins
**Type:** Opinion
**Published:** February 2, 2026
**Last Modified:** February 12, 2026

---

With over 90 different options, USC has a large number of diverse, interesting minors for students to choose from. However, many of the classes for these minors are rarely offered. This leads to the "false advertising" of minors—a phenomenon known in higher education as "zombie courses" or "ghost electives"—increasing the difficulty for students in fulfilling their minor requirements.

One example is the [professional writing minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/english-language-literature/professionals-writing-communication-minor/#requirementstext) in the English department. It requires four courses of a student's choosing from a list of 13 options. One of these courses is an independent study internship class, and among the other 12, only three are commonly offered.

[The English class records](https://sc.edu/study/colleges_schools/artsandsciences/english_language_and_literature/study/course_descriptions.php) going back to fall 2020 depict concerning data. Outside of the three commonly offered (advanced, business, and technical writing) English classes for the minor, the other options have collectively been offered only a few times since fall 2020. What is most concerning is that only two of the five speech classes available for the minor have been offered in the past 12 semesters, and for a total of only four times.

This also limits the [speech communication minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/english-language-literature/speech-communication-minor/), which requires at least three of the 300-level or above speech classes. Since fall 2020, only five of the 18 speech classes 300-level or above have been offered. Notably, the entire 2022-2023 school year and fall semesters of 2020, 2022, and 2023 offered none.

An average of only 1.5 speech courses 300+ were offered per semester since fall 2020. The only classes consistently offered were SPCH 387 and SPCH 399 (an independent research study). This severely limits speech communication minor students' choices and completion of requirements.

Outside of the humanities, this issue is seen, but to a lesser degree. [The climate and society minor,](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/geography/climate-society-minor/#requirementstext) which requires choosing four courses from 12 GEOG/ENVR classes, averages about three class offerings per semester from fall 2020 to spring 2026. While there was more variety, several advertised courses—GEOG 568, GEOG 335, and ENVR/GEOG 517—have not been offered once in the past six years.

The core issue is that this is "hidden information." Finding out how often classes are offered requires individually searching through past semesters or manually browsing Self Service Carolina's shopping cart records. At peer institutions like the University of Florida and Clemson University, this data is public and transparent. Their catalogs often include "Typically Offered" labels (e.g., "Offered Fall only" or "Offered even-numbered years") directly in the course description, allowing students to plan with clarity.

This scarcity isn't just an administrative oversight; it’s a symptom of larger systemic issues. With roughly 57% of USC faculty being non-tenure track or adjuncts, the university often lacks the stable, full-time staff needed to teach niche, upper-level electives. Furthermore, as state funding has dwindled—USC is now only about 13% state-funded—departments are pressured to prioritize high-enrollment general education courses over the specialized classes that make a minor worth pursuing.

The solution is simple: USC needs a "truth in advertising" policy for its academic bulletin. 

First, the Registrar should adopt a "Sunset Policy," similar to those at many Big Ten universities, where any course not taught in five years is automatically moved to an "inactive" status. Second, the university should implement "Expected Term" labels in the catalog. If a course is only offered "on demand" or "periodically," it should be clearly marked as such. Finally, departments with niche minors should be required to publish a two-year rotation plan so students can see exactly when their required courses will appear.

Students should focus on fulfilling their major requirements and only secondarily on their minor. Most choose a minor to supplement their learning or pursue a passion. Making the part of the university experience that should be simple—obtaining a minor—into a stressful and complicated process of hunting for "ghost" classes is detrimental to student success.

Ultimately, if niche minors are to be offered, their limitations need to be communicated. Clearly divulging information on courses that are rarely or never offered is vital to student decision-making. For students to get the most out of USC’s specialized programs, the university must clarify course availability so that a student's academic potential doesn't become an undesirable chore.
