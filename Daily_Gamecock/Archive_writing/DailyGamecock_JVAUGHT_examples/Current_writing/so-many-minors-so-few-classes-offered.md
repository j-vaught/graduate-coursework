# Column: USC Is Falsely Advertising Its Minors

**Author:** Legare Jenkins
**Type:** Opinion
**Published:** February 2, 2026
**Last Modified:** February 8, 2026

---

With over 90 different options, USC has a large number of diverse, interesting minors for students to choose from. However, many of the classes for these minors are rarely offered. This leads to false advertising of minors, increasing the difficulty for students in fulfilling their minor.

One example is the [professional writing minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/english-language-literature/professionals-writing-communication-minor/#requirementstext) in the English department. It requires 4 courses of a student's choosing from a list of 13 options. One of these courses is an independent study internship class, and among the other 12, only 3 are commonly offered.

[The English class records](https://sc.edu/study/colleges_schools/artsandsciences/english_language_and_literature/study/course_descriptions.php) going back to fall 2020 depict concerning data. Outside of the 3 commonly offered (advanced, business, and technical writing) English classes for the minor, the other 4 have collectively been offered only a few times since fall 2020. What is most concerning is that only 2 of the 5 speech classes available for the minor have been offered in the past 12 semesters, and for a total of only 4 times.

This also limits the [speech communication minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/english-language-literature/speech-communication-minor/), which requires at least 3 of the 300-level or above speech classes. Since fall 2020, only 5 of the 18 speech classes 300-level or above have been offered. Notably, the entire 2022-2023 school year and fall semesters of 2020, 2022, and 2023 offered none.

An average of only 1.5 speech courses 300+ were offered per semester since fall 2020. The only classes consistently offered were SPCH 387 and SPCH 399 (an independent research study). This severely limits speech communication minor students' choices and completion of requirements.

Outside of the humanities, this issue is seen, but to a lesser degree. [The climate and society minor,](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/geography/climate-society-minor/#requirementstext) which requires one to choose 4 courses from 12 GEOG/ENVR classes, averages about 3 class offerings per semester from fall 2020 to spring 2026. There was notably more course variety for this minor, with almost all courses listed being offered at some point from fall 2020 to this spring.

Though 3 classes for the minor: GEOG 568, GEOG 335, and ENVR/GEOG 517 have not been offered since fall 2020. Many other classes for the minor were only offered 2 or 3 of the past 12 semesters, making them inconsistent. While this is much better than the professional writing and speech communication minors, it still is not ideal, considering that about half of the advertised classes cannot be relied upon to be offered.

The core issue is that this is hidden information. Finding the data on how often classes are offered requires individually searching through past semesters on course offerings websites (if available) or manually browsing Self Service Carolina's class shopping cart records, which date back to 2013. The information may be there, but it is not quickly accessible.

When students sign up for a minor, they are likely to check the courses to fulfill it, not various past reservoirs from when they were offered. This leads to students signing up for minors that have become difficult to obtain over time or severely limited in their offerings. Seeing a list of 13 courses, but only about ⅓ of them being offered, creates student frustration.

For students who are only interested in a few of the classes listed for a minor, there is a large chance that they may never get to take any course they want. Students are then stuck, unable to take the classes that convinced them to declare the minor in the first place.

It makes sense that some classes are consistently only offered in certain semesters and some are prioritized over others. But for certain minors, classes are not merely inconsistent but are rarely or not offered entirely in recent semesters, creating a false perception in students of what the minor will be.

The solution is simple. Communicate to students more openly whether classes for unique minors are or are not offered often. On the list of classes counting toward niche minors, designate the rarely offered (only once every few semesters) classes with asterisks, and designate those that have not been offered in the past 5 years with a double asterisk, or into an entirely new section. This way, students are immediately aware of the likelihood of getting into the classes and knowledgable of potential risks upfront.

The university has to base class offerings on demand and general requirements. While this system is effective for supporting majors and some minors, it fails to serve more niche minors. Professors and university time may need to be allocated toward higher demand courses, but this allocation of fewer resources to certain classes should be communicated, not hidden.

Students should focus on fulfilling their major requirements and only secondarily on their minor. Most students choose a minor to supplement their learning, pursue another interest, or for personal enjoyment. Making the part of the university experience that should be simple, obtaining a minor, into a stressful and complicated process, is detrimental to student success.

Ultimately, if niche minors are to be offered, their limitations need to be communicated. Clearly divulging information on courses that are rarely or never offered to students would be vital to their decision-making process in choosing a minor. For the students to get the most out of the university's specialized minors, it would be best to clarify course availability so their exciting potential does not become an undesirable chore.
