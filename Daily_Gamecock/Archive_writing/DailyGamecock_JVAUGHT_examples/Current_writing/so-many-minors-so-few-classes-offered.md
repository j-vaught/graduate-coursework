# Column: USC Is Falsely Advertising Its Minors

**Author:** Legare Jenkins
**Type:** Opinion
**Published:** February 2, 2026
**Last Modified:** February 12, 2026

---

With over 106 different minors, USC has a large number of diverse and interesting options for students to choose from. However, analysis of five years of grade spread data reveals a troubling reality. Eleven of these minors cannot be completed because required courses have never been offered. Another 16 minors have required courses offered less than 30 percent of the time. Across all minors, 17 courses have never appeared in teaching schedules. Another 42 are offered less than once every five semesters. Students are signing up for degree requirements that do not exist.

The Audio Recording Minor illustrates this problem starkly. USC's bulletin lists six required courses. But grade spread data from 18 semesters reveals the gap between what is advertised and what exists. One required course, MUSC 571 (Digital Audio Technology), has never been offered in five years. Zero semesters.

A second required course, MUSC 566 (Fundamentals of Sound Use for Media), was offered once in Spring 2023 with 11 students. A third course, MUSC 567 (Recording Studio Techniques), was offered only twice in Fall 2024 and Fall 2025, totaling 24 students across five years. A student pursuing this minor faces an impossible task.

The Audio Recording Minor is not an outlier. It is the pattern. The [professional writing minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/english-language-literature/professionals-writing-communication-minor/#requirementstext) lists ENGL 493 as required but it has never been offered. The [Jewish Studies minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/interdisciplinary-studies/jewish-studies-minor/) requires JSTU 373, JSTU 381, and JSTU 382. None have been offered in 15 semesters. The [Latin minor](https://academicbulletins.sc.edu/undergraduate/arts-sciences/languages-literatures-cultures/latin-minor/) requires LATN 301, never taught.

Ten more minors face the same problem. These programs exist in catalogs. Students declare them. But required courses do not exist in teaching schedules.

The scope of this problem extends systematically across the minor system. Analysis reveals 17 courses never offered and 42 courses offered less than 30 percent of the time. Additionally, 39 minors face critical risk and 35 more face high risk due to course scarcity.

Some departments are particularly affected. Foreign Language Education relies on FORL courses never offered. Jewish Studies requires courses never offered. Arabic Studies courses are offered only 15.6 percent of the time.

The core issue is that this information is hidden from students. When students sign up for a minor, they check the courses required to fulfill it, not five years of grade spread data. This leads to students making commitments to minors that have become difficult to obtain or severely limited in their offerings. Seeing a list of required courses on the official bulletin, students cannot know that one-fifth of USC's advertised minors are mathematically impossible to complete. Looking at 13 courses for the professional writing minor, a student cannot know that one of them has never been offered.

For passionate students who declare based on specific courses, there is a real risk they will never take those classes. They become trapped, committed to a degree requirement they cannot fulfill. The information gap is the trap. Finding out how often classes are offered requires digging through past semesters or manually browsing Self Service Carolina records. Peer institutions like the [University of Arizona](https://catalog.arizona.edu/courses) include course availability in their descriptions. Students know immediately whether courses are offered fall, spring, annually, or only in specific years. USC does not.

This scarcity is not merely an administrative oversight. It is structural and intentional. State appropriations account for just 16.4 percent of the USC Columbia budget for FY2025. Departments prioritize high-enrollment courses over specialized classes that make minors worthwhile. But this choice should come with honesty, not hidden information.

It makes sense that some classes are prioritized over others. For certain minors, classes are rarely or never offered, creating false promises about requirements. The solution is straightforward. Communicate openly whether classes are offered often.

On niche minor lists, mark rarely offered courses with asterisks. Use double asterisks for courses not offered in five years. Better yet, create a separate section for these courses. This way, students know upfront which classes are real and which are aspirational.

For the 11 minors where completion is currently impossible, affected students deserve more than transparency. They deserve notification and immediate options. If USC advertises a course in a minor's requirements, students should expect to be able to take it. If USC cannot teach the required courses, students should be allowed to substitute approved alternatives or withdraw from the minor entirely without academic penalty.

The university has to base class offerings on demand and general requirements. While this system is effective for supporting majors and some minors, it fails to serve more niche minors. Professors and university time may need to be allocated toward higher demand courses, but this allocation of fewer resources to certain classes should be communicated, not hidden.

Students should focus on fulfilling their major requirements and only secondarily on their minor. Most students choose a minor to supplement their learning, pursue another interest, or for personal enjoyment [OBSERVATION: Can cite student survey data if available, or mark as opinion-based observation]. The part of college that should feel achievable and exciting should not become a source of frustration and broken promises.

Ultimately, if niche minors are to be offered, their limitations need to be communicated honestly. Clearly divulging information on courses that are rarely or never offered is not optional. It is essential to any student's decision-making process in choosing a minor. For students to get the most out of the university's specialized minors, USC must clarify course availability so their exciting potential does not become an impossible chore.
