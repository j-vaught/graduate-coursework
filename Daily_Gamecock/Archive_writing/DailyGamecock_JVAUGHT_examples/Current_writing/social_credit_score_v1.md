# Column: America needs a government-backed social credit score

**Published:** January 2026
**Link:** [placeholder]

I know what you're thinking. A government social credit system? That's authoritarian. That's dystopian. That's China.

But here's the uncomfortable truth: You already live under a social credit system. You just don't call it that. And the version we have now is actually worse than the one we love to fear.

Right now, as you read this, dozens of companies are scoring your trustworthiness, your reliability, your worthiness to participate in society. They're deciding whether you can rent an apartment, get a job, qualify for insurance, or even keep your bank account. The difference between their system and China's is simple: Ours is privatized, opaque, and accountable to no one. China's at least pretends to have rules.

Let me prove it to you. When's the last time you checked your credit score? That three-digit number, calculated by algorithms you can't see, using criteria that change without notice, maintained by three private corporations that [received 2.7 million complaints last year alone](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-releases-report-detailing-consumer-complaint-response-deficiencies-of-the-big-three-credit-bureaus/). That score determines whether you pay 3% or 7% on a mortgage. We're talking about [$165 per month difference](https://themortgagereports.com/87625/mortgage-rates-by-credit-score) on a median home loan. Over 30 years, a bad credit score costs you [nearly $60,000 in extra interest](https://themortgagereports.com/87625/mortgage-rates-by-credit-score) for the exact same house.

But credit scores are just the beginning. If you're a student at USC looking for your first apartment in Columbia, you're not just competing on credit. You're facing SafeRent, CoreLogic, and other tenant screening companies that use algorithmic risk scores. SafeRent just [settled a discrimination lawsuit](https://www.cohenmilstein.com/rental-applicants-using-housing-vouchers-settle-ground-breaking-discrimination-class-action-against-saferent-solutions/) for systematically scoring Black and Latino renters lower than white applicants with identical financial histories. The algorithm was racist. No one knew until lawyers spent years fighting for the data.

## The invisible gatekeepers

Here's where it gets worse. [Ninety-five percent of auto insurance companies](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/credit-based-insurance-scores-impacts-consumers-automobile-insurance-report-congress-federal-trade/p044804facta_report_credit-based_insurance_scores.pdf) use credit-based insurance scores that have nothing to do with your driving record. You could be a perfect driver with a clean record, but if you missed a credit card payment during COVID, you're paying hundreds more per year for car insurance. The [Consumer Federation of America found](https://consumerfed.org/reports/the-one-hundred-percent-penalty-how-auto-insurers-use-of-credit-information-increases-premiums-for-safe-drivers-and-perpetuates-racial-inequality/) that a driver with excellent credit and one accident pays less than a perfect driver with poor credit. Read that again. Your financial history matters more than whether you crash cars.

Looking for a job after graduation? Join the club. [Ninety-five percent of employers conduct background checks](https://www.securitymagazine.com/articles/89214-background-screening-utilized-by-95-of-us-employers). About [70 million Americans have criminal records](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/just-facts-many-americans-have-criminal-records-college-diplomas) that show up in these searches, affecting everything from entry-level retail to professional careers. Many of these records contain errors. A study by the [National Consumer Law Center found](https://nclc.org/issues/rpt-broken-records-redux.html) that a significant percentage of background check reports contain inaccuracies, from misspellings to entirely wrong criminal records. You could lose a job opportunity because someone with a similar name committed a crime in another state. Good luck disputing it before the employer moves to the next candidate.

And we haven't even talked about the financial system's power to unperson you. Ask [Nigel Farage about getting "debanked"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage_Coutts_bank_scandal) in the UK for his political views. It's happening here too. [Operation Chokepoint 2.0](https://decrypt.co/293947/operation-chokepoint-2-0-crypto-founders-silenced-by-secret-debanking) has seen cryptocurrency entrepreneurs, legal cannabis businesses, and politically controversial figures suddenly lose access to banking services with no explanation, no appeal, no recourse. Once the banks decide you're too risky or too controversial, you're functionally excluded from the modern economy. Try getting paid, paying rent, or buying groceries without a bank account.

## The platform panopticon

Then there's the digital layer. Every major platform uses opaque content moderation algorithms that can shadow ban your posts, throttle your reach, or delete your account entirely. [About 10% of social media users](https://cdt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/CDT-Social-Media-Shadowbanning-Survey-FINAL-FOR-publication.pdf) have been shadow banned without knowing it, posting into a void while the algorithm decides they're not worth amplifying. For students building careers in journalism, content creation, or digital marketing, an unexplained platform ban can destroy years of work. No trial. No explanation. No alternative.

Instagram's algorithm decides whether your portfolio reaches potential employers. LinkedIn's system determines whether recruiters see your profile. TikTok's black box controls whether your content gets promoted or buried. These aren't public squares anymore. They're private systems of social control, and they're answering to advertisers and executives, not citizens.

The data broker industry has gone even further. LexisNexis maintains a ["Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange" database](https://wallethub.com/edu/ci/clue-report/10595) tracking your insurance claims history, which insurers use to predict your risk and set your premiums. Most people don't know it exists. You can't opt out. Data brokers sell your location history, purchase patterns, and browsing behavior to anyone willing to pay. [CoreLogic sells "predictive risk scores"](https://www.upturn.org/work/response-to-the-ftcs-request-for-information-on-tenant-screening-technologies/) to landlords based on data you never consented to provide.

This is social credit. It's just fragmented across hundreds of companies, each with its own algorithms, its own errors, its own biases, and absolutely zero democratic accountability.

## A better authoritarianism

So here's my proposal: If we're going to live under social credit, let's make it constitutional. Let's make it transparent. Let's make it accountable.

A government-backed social credit system would actually be subject to the Fourth Amendment. Private companies aren't. They can collect, analyze, and sell your data without warrants, without probable cause, without constitutional limits. A government system would face immediate legal challenges if it violated due process or equal protection. Private credit bureaus don't have to worry about the Equal Protection Clause. That's why [ProPublica found](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing) that COMPAS, the criminal risk algorithm used in sentencing, rated Black defendants as twice as likely to reoffend even when they weren't.

Government systems are subject to Freedom of Information Act requests. You could demand to see the algorithm. You could find out why you were scored down. You could challenge errors through administrative law procedures that actually exist. Try getting Equifax to explain why your score dropped 40 points. Try suing them when they refuse. You'll spend years in arbitration clauses you never agreed to read.

Most importantly, you can vote. If the government creates a social credit policy you hate, you can organize, protest, elect different representatives, and change the law. When was the last time you voted for the CEO of TransUnion? When did you get to weigh in on insurance algorithm updates? Never. You're not the customer. You're the product.

China's system is transparent about one thing: It exists. It has published rules. You know you're being scored. Ours pretends to be free market innovation while doing the exact same thing with less accountability. We've outsourced authoritarianism to avoid calling it what it is.

## The devil you can regulate

I'm not saying a government social credit system would be perfect. I'm saying it would be better than the privatized nightmare we already inhabit. It would be subject to constitutional constraints, democratic pressure, and legal challenges. It would have to publish its criteria, justify its decisions, and provide appeals processes. It couldn't legally discriminate by race, and if it did, you could sue under civil rights law instead of hoping a class action clears arbitration in five years.

The system we have now is social credit with none of the safeguards and all of the control. We just call it innovation instead of authoritarianism. We let algorithms decide who deserves housing, employment, insurance, and banking, and we act surprised when those decisions replicate every bias baked into American inequality.

You already have a social credit score. Actually, you have dozens. You just can't see them, can't challenge them, and can't vote them out. Maybe it's time we demanded something better than privatized social control dressed up as freedom. Maybe it's time we admitted that if we're going to be scored, judged, and sorted by algorithms, we deserve the protection of democracy, transparency, and constitutional law.

Or we can keep pretending we're different from China while private companies do the same thing with better PR.
