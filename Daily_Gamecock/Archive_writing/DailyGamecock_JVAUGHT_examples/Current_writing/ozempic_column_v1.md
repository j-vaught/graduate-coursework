# Column: Ozempic already works at UofSC — if your parents can afford it

A month's supply of Wegovy costs $1,349. The university student health plan does not cover it. If your family carries private insurance from a large employer, you might pay a $50 copay. If you rely on the BlueCross BlueShield plan the university offers through Academic HealthPlans, you pay full price or you go without.

This is not a hypothetical. One in eight American adults now takes a GLP-1 medication. Usage among 18-to-25-year-olds increased 659% for women and 481% for men between 2020 and 2023. More than 60,000 young adults filled GLP-1 prescriptions in 2023, up from 8,700 three years earlier. These drugs are already on this campus. The insurance exclusion does not prevent GLP-1 use at Carolina. It ensures that only students with wealthy parents benefit.

The medical debate is over. The American Medical Association classified obesity as a chronic disease in 2013. The World Health Organization defines it as "a chronic, relapsing disease arising from complex interactions between genetics, neurobiology, eating behaviours, and the broader environment." A January 2025 Lancet Commission introduced a clinical framework distinguishing obesity as a systemic disease state, not a lifestyle failure. Six leading medical organizations jointly declared that obesity "requires lifelong care."

The FDA agrees. In the past 14 months, it approved GLP-1 drugs for obstructive sleep apnea, chronic kidney disease, and liver fibrosis — conditions no one dismisses as cosmetic. Semaglutide reduces major cardiovascular events by 20% in people with obesity who do not have diabetes. The SELECT trial proved that. These are not vanity drugs. They are cardiovascular medicine that happens to cause weight loss.

Yet the student health plan treats them as elective. Consider what it does cover. Smoking cessation medications — all seven FDA-approved options — are mandated under the Affordable Care Act with zero cost-sharing. Addiction treatment, including methadone and buprenorphine, is required under mental health parity laws. Blood pressure medication, statins, insulin, CPAP machines — the plan covers the consequences of obesity while refusing to cover the class of drugs proven to address the condition itself. This is like covering chemotherapy but refusing to pay for the screening that catches cancer early.

The university did not make this decision in a vacuum. It outsourced it.

Here is how the system actually works. Novo Nordisk sets the list price of Wegovy at $1,349 per month. Eli Lilly prices Zepbound at $1,086. Combined, tirzepatide products generated $10.1 billion for Lilly in a single quarter — Q3 2025 — making it the world's best-selling drug. Novo Nordisk posted $46.7 billion in revenue over 12 months. These companies are not struggling to fund research. They are extracting maximum value from a captive market.

Insurance companies respond predictably. BlueCross BlueShield, Cigna, UnitedHealthcare, and Harvard Pilgrim all restricted or eliminated GLP-1 coverage for weight loss effective January 1, 2026. The industry rationale is straightforward: GLP-1 drugs now account for 10.5% of total annual insurance claims, up from 6.9% in 2022. One Minnesota school district found that GLP-1 prescriptions represented 2% of its prescriptions but 56% of total pharmacy costs. Covering these drugs raises premiums between 5.3% and 13.8%. Insurers decided the math did not work.

The university plan follows this industry default. Not because administrators evaluated the medical evidence and concluded students do not need these drugs. Not because a committee weighed the equity implications. Because the insurance carrier drew a line and the university accepted it. The result is a campus health policy shaped not by student needs or medical science but by a cost dispute between two industries in which students have no voice.

South Carolina's state Medicaid program made the same calculation. As of January 1, 2026, it stopped covering GLP-1 drugs for weight loss entirely — one of four states to eliminate coverage since October 2025. Only 13 state Medicaid programs still cover these medications for obesity. The pattern is consistent: the populations with the highest obesity rates and the fewest resources lose access first.

South Carolina's adult obesity rate is 32.3%, the 12th highest in the nation. Among 18-to-25-year-olds in the state, 19.5% meet the clinical threshold for obesity. Nationally, obesity prevalence in this age group rose from 15.7% to 20.1% over the past decade, with researchers noting that "the most rapid increases are now occurring among younger adults." These are the students walking into the Thomson Student Health Center. The university knows who they are. It has chosen not to cover the most effective treatment available to them.

The students who can afford it find workarounds. Until mid-2025, compounding pharmacies offered cheaper semaglutide alternatives. Then the FDA resolved the drug shortage, ended its enforcement discretion, and issued warning letters to more than 40 telehealth-based compounding operations in a single September sweep. The affordable back door closed. What remains is a front door priced at $1,349 per month — open to students whose families can pay, closed to everyone else.

Thirty-seven percent of Gen Z say they plan to include GLP-1 medications in their wellness goals. The demand exists. The medical justification exists. The drugs exist. What does not exist is a university willing to negotiate with its insurance carrier on behalf of students who cannot negotiate for themselves.

The Trump administration managed to secure a deal. In November 2025, agreements with Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk established Medicare pricing at $245 per month with a $50 copay for eligible beneficiaries, effective mid-2026. If the federal government can negotiate GLP-1 pricing for 65 million Medicare enrollees, a university can negotiate formulary inclusion for its student plan. The question is whether it considers that negotiation worth having.

This is not a request for the university to buy every student Ozempic. Prior authorization requirements, BMI thresholds, step therapy protocols — these are standard tools insurers use to manage access responsibly. Peer institutions and large employers have implemented coverage with these guardrails without destroying their budgets. The choice is not between unlimited coverage and total exclusion. It is between a policy that acknowledges medical reality and one that pretends GLP-1 drugs are cosmetic while covering every downstream condition they prevent.

The student health plan is not a neutral document. It reflects what the university believes its students deserve. Right now, it says students deserve insulin but not the drug that might eliminate their need for it. It says students deserve blood pressure medication but not the treatment that addresses why their blood pressure is high. It says obesity is a problem students should manage on their own — unless their parents can write a check.

Novo Nordisk set the price. BlueCross drew the line. The university accepted it. You pay the consequences. That is not a healthcare policy. It is an abdication.
