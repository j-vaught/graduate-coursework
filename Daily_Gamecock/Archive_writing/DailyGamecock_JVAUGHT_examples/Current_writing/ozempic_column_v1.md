# Column: Ozempic already works at UofSC — if your parents can afford it

A month's supply of Wegovy costs $1,349. The university student health plan does not cover it. Graduate students, graduate assistants, medical students, and international students are automatically enrolled in the mandatory BlueCross BlueShield plan administered by Academic HealthPlans — $1,596.33 for fall, $2,183.67 for spring and summer, $3,780 per year — and that plan explicitly excludes "Weight Loss Programs" from covered services. If your family carries private insurance from a large employer, you might pay a $50 copay. If you rely on the plan the university requires you to carry, you pay full price or you go without.

One in eight American adults now takes a GLP-1 medication. Usage among 18-to-25-year-olds increased 659% for women and 481% for men between 2020 and 2023. More than 60,000 young adults filled GLP-1 prescriptions in 2023, up from 8,700 three years earlier. These drugs are already on campuses like this one. The insurance exclusion does not prevent GLP-1 use at Carolina. It ensures that only students whose families carry private insurance that covers them benefit.

The medical debate is over. The American Medical Association classified obesity as a chronic disease in 2013. The World Health Organization defines it as "a chronic, relapsing disease arising from complex interactions between genetics, neurobiology, eating behaviours, and the broader environment." A January 2025 Lancet Commission introduced a clinical framework distinguishing obesity as a systemic disease state, not a lifestyle failure. Leading medical organizations — including the Obesity Society, the Obesity Medicine Association, and the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery — jointly declared that obesity "requires lifelong care."

The FDA agrees. Since late 2024, it has approved GLP-1 drugs for obstructive sleep apnea, chronic kidney disease, and liver fibrosis — conditions no one dismisses as cosmetic. Semaglutide reduces major cardiovascular events by 20% in people with obesity who do not have diabetes. The SELECT trial proved that. These are not vanity drugs. They are cardiovascular medicine that happens to cause weight loss.

Yet the student health plan treats them as elective. The Summary of Benefits and Coverage lists "Weight Loss Programs" alongside cosmetic surgery and acupuncture under "Services Your Plan Generally Does NOT Cover." The plan carries a $500 in-network deductible, a $9,200 out-of-pocket maximum, and a separate $100 prescription drug deductible — standard PPO architecture. It covers generic drugs at a $20 copay, preferred brands at $40, non-preferred brands at $100, and specialty drugs at $100. Consider what it does cover. Smoking cessation medications — all seven FDA-approved options — are mandated under the Affordable Care Act with zero cost-sharing. Addiction treatment, including methadone and buprenorphine, is required under mental health parity laws. Blood pressure medication, statins, insulin, CPAP machines — the plan covers the consequences of obesity while refusing to cover the class of drugs proven to address the condition itself. This is like covering dialysis but refusing the drug that prevents kidney failure.

The university did not make this decision in a vacuum. It outsourced it.

Here is how the system actually works. Novo Nordisk sets the list price of Wegovy at $1,349 per month. Eli Lilly prices Zepbound at $1,086. Combined, tirzepatide products generated $10.1 billion for Lilly in a single quarter — Q3 2025 — making it the world's best-selling drug. Novo Nordisk posted $46.7 billion in revenue over 12 months. These are not R&D costs passed to patients — they are profit margins defended by 29 patents extending through 2033.

Insurance companies respond predictably. BlueCross BlueShield, Cigna, and UnitedHealthcare all restricted or eliminated GLP-1 coverage for weight loss effective January 1, 2026. The industry rationale is straightforward: GLP-1 drugs now account for 10.5% of total annual insurance claims, up from 6.9% in 2022. One Minnesota school district found that GLP-1 prescriptions represented 2% of its prescriptions but 56% of total pharmacy costs. Covering these drugs raises premiums between 5.3% and 13.8%. Insurers decided the math did not work.

The university plan follows this industry default. Students paying $3,780 a year for mandatory coverage — or $5,187 for the voluntary plan available to undergraduates — receive a policy whose excluded-services list was written by the carrier, not by the university. There is no public evidence that administrators evaluated the medical case or weighed equity implications before accepting the carrier's formulary exclusion. The result is a campus health policy shaped not by student needs or medical science but by a cost dispute between two industries in which students have no voice.

South Carolina's state Medicaid program made the same calculation. As of January 1, 2026, it stopped covering GLP-1 drugs for weight loss entirely — one of four states to eliminate coverage since October 2025. Only 13 state Medicaid programs still cover these medications for obesity. The pattern is consistent: the populations with the highest obesity rates and the fewest resources lose access first. Mississippi, the state with the nation's highest adult obesity rate, never covered GLP-1s for weight loss at all.

South Carolina's adult obesity rate is 32.3%, the 12th highest in the nation. Among 18-to-25-year-olds in the state, 19.5% meet the clinical threshold for obesity. Nationally, obesity prevalence in this age group rose from 15.7% to 20.1% over the past decade, with researchers noting that "the most rapid increases are now occurring among younger adults." These are the students walking into the Thomson Student Health Center. The university knows who they are. It has chosen not to cover the most effective pharmaceutical treatment available to them.

The students who can afford it find workarounds. Until mid-2025, compounding pharmacies offered cheaper semaglutide alternatives. Then the FDA resolved the drug shortage, ended its enforcement discretion, and issued warning letters to more than 50 telehealth-based compounding operations in a single September sweep. The affordable back door closed. What remains is a front door priced at $1,349 per month — open to students whose families can pay, closed to everyone else.

The demand exists — a 659% increase in usage among young women makes that clear. The medical justification exists. The drugs exist. What does not exist is a university willing to negotiate with its insurance carrier on behalf of students who cannot negotiate for themselves.

The Trump administration managed to secure a deal. In November 2025, agreements with Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk established Medicare pricing at $245 per month with a $50 copay for eligible beneficiaries, effective mid-2026. A university plan lacks federal negotiating power, but the principle holds: if the federal government can negotiate GLP-1 pricing for 65 million Medicare enrollees, a university can negotiate formulary inclusion for its student plan. The question is whether it considers that negotiation worth having.

This is not a request for the university to buy every student Ozempic. Prior authorization requirements, BMI thresholds, step therapy protocols — these are standard tools insurers use to manage access responsibly. Large employers — 43% of firms with 5,000 or more workers — have implemented coverage with these guardrails without destroying their budgets. The choice is not between unlimited coverage and total exclusion. It is between a policy that acknowledges medical reality and one that pretends GLP-1 drugs are cosmetic while covering every downstream condition they prevent.

The student health plan is not a neutral document. It reflects what the university believes its students deserve. Right now, it says students deserve insulin but not the drug that might eliminate their need for it. It says students deserve blood pressure medication but not the treatment that addresses why their blood pressure is high. It says obesity is a problem students should manage on their own — unless their parents can write a check.

Novo Nordisk set the price. BlueCross drew the line. The university accepted it. You pay the consequences. That is not a healthcare policy. It is an abdication.
