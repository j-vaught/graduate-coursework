# State-Assisted, State-Controlled

*By J.C. Vaught*

South Carolina provides 13% of USC's Columbia campus budget. Tuition and fees cover more than 50%. State funding remains nearly 40% below 2008 levels, adjusted for inflation, despite a full economic recovery. USC's own government relations office puts it plainly: public higher education in South Carolina is "state assisted rather than state funded."

So here is the question every tuition-paying student should be asking: if you and your family are covering most of the bill, why does the General Assembly get to run the university?

## What 13% Buys the Legislature

Start with governance. The legislature elects 16 of USC's 20 Board of Trustees members — one from each judicial circuit, chosen by majority vote of the General Assembly. The governor appoints one more. That leaves three ex officio seats. The people who actually fund this university — students, families, federal research agencies — have no direct representation on the board that governs it.

Then there is tuition. South Carolina has held in-state undergraduate tuition flat for eight consecutive years at $6,344 per semester, enabled by state tuition mitigation funds. That sounds generous until you learn the state provided $65 million for mitigation in 2025-2026 when universities said they needed $130 million to fully cover costs. University presidents have publicly called the freeze "not sustainable." The state is buying a talking point on the cheap, and students will eventually pay the difference.

Now add curriculum. Pending bills S.368 and H.3927, both in committee with Republican supermajorities in each chamber, would require the State Auditor to conduct DEI compliance audits of every public university at least once every four years. The bills define "DEI" so broadly that compliance reviews could reach student support services, university bookstores, and academic programs that study inequality. If the State Auditor finds a violation, the institution gets 180 days to cure it. If it doesn't, the State Fiscal Accountability Authority — the same body that approved $350 million in Williams-Brice Stadium renovation bonds last August — "may direct the State Treasurer to withhold all future distributions until the violation is cured." Both bills explicitly reference President Trump's Executive Order 14173.

All future distributions. For a definition of DEI that could cover a bookstore display.

## The Audit

None of this means the state has no legitimate oversight role. It does. And the December 2024 Legislative Audit Council report on USC's Office of Economic Engagement proved why.

The LAC found $1.7 million in questionable transactions from a $6 million federal COVID relief grant. Two Columbia computer labs funded by the grant never opened to the public — $635,600 gone. A $400,000 marketing contract went to a PR firm whose former CEO was a personal friend of OEE leadership; in 2023 the firm produced seven deliverables and two press releases. Eight employees signed federal time-and-effort certifications for grant work they told auditors they never performed. Someone bought Apple Watches for Palmetto College IT staff with computer lab money. The office ran over budget in five of six years studied.

That is real waste, and USC deserved the scrutiny. President Amiridis called the expenditures "allowable and appropriate," then revised the grant application to swap them out for "other legitimate expenses" — an argument that contradicts itself. If the spending was appropriate, why replace it?

## The Pattern

But look at what happens next. The audit found $1.7 million in mismanagement in one administrative office. The political response is not proportional oversight — it is permanent expansion of control.

Senator Josh Kimbrell, who helped initiate the audit and is now running for governor in 2026, called it "a very opaque way of doing things with little oversight." Senator Dick Harpootlian said he wants "random audits of all state agencies" and warned, "I'm afraid this is not an isolated problem." The DEI bills would layer compliance audits on top of the financial audits already required by federal law. Florida's governor has already launched a "DOGE" task force to audit every state university. Texas ordered curriculum audits statewide. South Carolina is building the same apparatus — maximum oversight, declining investment.

The response to a $1.7 million scandal at one office is never more funding. It is always more control.

## Who Actually Funds This University?

Compare what South Carolina contributes to what it demands. The state provides 13% of USC's budget but elects 80% of its governing board, dictates whether tuition can rise, is building a DEI compliance regime that could reach into classrooms and bookstores, and holds the power to cut off all distributions if it doesn't like what it finds.

Meanwhile, students and families provide more than half of operating revenue through tuition. Federal agencies fund the research enterprise — USC crossed $1 billion in research expenditures in FY2023 and received $70.3 million from NIH alone in 2024. Sixteen USC grants received stop-work notifications in 2025, possibly over DEI components. The state's contribution is a policy lever, not a funding source.

And while the legislature debates how to audit what professors teach, SFAA approved $350 million in stadium bonds without comparable scrutiny. The tuition freeze that the state takes credit for is underfunded by half. Graduate students and researchers absorb the compliance costs of every new audit requirement, every new certification, every new reporting layer — costs that nationally consume 3-11% of university operating budgets and take researchers away from the work that actually generates revenue and knowledge.

If South Carolina wants to control USC like a state agency, it should fund it like one. At 13%, the state is not a patron — it is a silent partner with a controlling interest. Students deserve to know who is actually writing the checks and who is calling the shots, because right now those are two very different groups of people.
