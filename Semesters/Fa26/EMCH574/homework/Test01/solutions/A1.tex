I take positive \(\theta\) in the direction shown and define \(u=L\theta\) as the tangential arc displacement, which gives \(\ddot u=L\ddot\theta\). I assume a point mass, a massless inextensible rod or taut string of length \(L\), a fixed frictionless pivot, uniform gravity, planar motion, and negligible air resistance.

The weight is \(mg\), and the tension \(T\) has no tangential component. Therefore, the tangential force is the restoring component of gravity,

\[\boxed{\sum F_u=-mg\sin\theta=m\ddot u=mL\ddot\theta}\qquad\text{(A.1.3)}\]

For small angular displacement in radians, \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\). Substituting this approximation gives the equation of motion

\[\boxed{mL\ddot\theta+mg\theta=0}\qquad\text{(A.1.4)}\]

Dividing by \(mL\) gives the governing equation

\[\boxed{\ddot\theta+\frac{g}{L}\theta=0}\qquad\text{(A.1.5)}\]

Since the governing equation is linear with constant coefficients, the trial response \(\theta=e^{st}\) applies. Then \(\ddot\theta=s^2e^{st}\), so substitution gives

\[\left(s^2+\frac{g}{L}\right)e^{st}=0.\]

Because \(e^{st}\neq0\), the characteristic equation is

\[\boxed{s^2+\frac{g}{L}=0}\qquad\text{(A.1.7)}\]

Since \(g/L>0\), solving this equation gives purely imaginary roots. Therefore, the answer to item 8 is

\[\boxed{\text{Imaginary}}\qquad\text{(A.1.8.b)}\]

Defining the natural angular frequency by \(\omega_n^2=g/L\) gives

\[\boxed{\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}}\qquad\text{(A.1.9)}\]

Using this definition, the roots become

\[\boxed{s_1=i\omega_n,\qquad s_2=-i\omega_n}\qquad\text{(A.1.10)}\]

The two roots produce the exponential angular response

\[\boxed{\theta(t)=D_1e^{i\omega_nt}+D_2e^{-i\omega_nt}}\qquad\text{(A.1.11)}\]

Using Euler's identity, the same response can be written as

\[\boxed{\theta(t)=A_\theta\cos(\omega_nt)+B_\theta\sin(\omega_nt)}\qquad\text{(A.1.12)}\]

Because \(\omega_n=2\pi f_n\) and \(f_n=1/\tau\), the frequency and period are

\[\boxed{f_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}}\qquad\text{(A.1.13)}\]

\[\boxed{\tau=\frac{1}{f_n}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_n}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}}\qquad\text{(A.1.14)}\]

Multiplying the angular response by \(L\) gives the particle-motion response. In exponential form, this is

\[\boxed{u(t)=C_1e^{i\omega_nt}+C_2e^{-i\omega_nt}}\qquad\text{(A.1.15.a)}\]

Euler's identity also gives the trigonometric forms

\[\boxed{u(t)=A\cos(\omega_nt)+B\sin(\omega_nt)
=C\sin(\omega_nt+\varphi)}\qquad\text{(A.1.15.b)}\]

For real \(A\) and \(B\), the amplitude and phase are \(C=\sqrt{A^2+B^2}\) and \(\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}(A,B)\).

Differentiating the trigonometric response and evaluating it at \(t=0\) gives

\[u(0)=A=u_0,\qquad \dot u(0)=\omega_nB=v_0,\qquad v_0=\dot u_0.\]

With initial displacement only, \(v_0=0\), so

\[\boxed{u(t)=u_0\cos(\omega_nt)}\qquad\text{(A.1.16.i)}\]

With initial velocity only, \(u_0=0\), so

\[\boxed{u(t)=\frac{v_0}{\omega_n}\sin(\omega_nt)}\qquad\text{(A.1.16.ii)}\]

With both initial conditions applied, the response is

\[\boxed{u(t)=u_0\cos(\omega_nt)+\frac{v_0}{\omega_n}\sin(\omega_nt)}\qquad\text{(A.1.16.iii)}\]

The corresponding exponential constants are

\[C_1=\frac12\left(u_0-\frac{iv_0}{\omega_n}\right),\qquad
C_2=\frac12\left(u_0+\frac{iv_0}{\omega_n}\right).\]

The mass cancels when the governing equation is divided by \(m\), so the ideal small-angle pendulum frequency depends on \(g\) and \(L\), but not on \(m\).
