\textbf{B.6.1. Assumptions.} The model has one translational degree of freedom $u(t)$, a point mass $m$, a linear spring with stiffness $k$, and a viscous damper with coefficient $c$. The support is fixed, the motion is small, and the applied force $F(t)$ acts in the positive $x$ direction. Spring and damper forces oppose the motion, and the parameters $m$, $c$, and $k$ remain constant.

\textbf{B.6.2. Free-body diagram and force sum.} Take positive $x$ to the right. The applied force is $F(t)$, while the spring and damper reactions act to the left. Therefore,

\[
\boxed{\sum F_x=F(t)-c\dot{u}(t)-ku(t)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.2)}
\]

The corresponding isolated mass is shown below.

\caption{B.6.2. Free-body diagram for the forced spring-mass-damper system.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{B.6.3. Newton's law of motion.} Newton's law relates the net force to the mass acceleration.

\[
\boxed{\sum F_x=m\ddot{u}(t)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.3)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.4. Equation of motion.} Equating the force sum to the inertial term gives

\[
F(t)-c\dot{u}(t)-ku(t)=m\ddot{u}(t).
\]

Moving the spring and damper terms to the left gives the governing ODE.

\[
\boxed{m\ddot{u}(t)+c\dot{u}(t)+ku(t)=F(t)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.4)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.5. Harmonic excitation and division by mass.} Let the applied force be represented by a complex harmonic function,

\[
F(t)=\hat{F}e^{i\omega t}.
\]

Substitution into the equation of motion and division by $m$ gives

\[
\boxed{\ddot{u}+\frac{c}{m}\dot{u}+\frac{k}{m}u=\frac{\hat{F}}{m}e^{i\omega t}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.5)}
\]

The physical force and displacement are obtained by taking the real parts of the complex expressions.

\textbf{B.6.6. Normalized nonhomogeneous ODE.} Define the normalized force amplitude and the natural frequency by

\[
\hat{f}=\frac{\hat{F}}{m},
\qquad
\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.
\]

The damping ratio is

\[
\zeta=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{mk}}=\frac{c}{2m\omega_n},
\qquad
\frac{c}{m}=2\zeta\omega_n,
\qquad
\frac{k}{m}=\omega_n^2.
\]

Substitution into the divided equation produces the normalized forced-vibration ODE.

\[
\boxed{\ddot{u}+2\zeta\omega_n\dot{u}+\omega_n^2u=\hat{f}e^{i\omega t}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.6)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.7. General solution.} The right-hand side is nonzero, so the solution is the sum of a complementary solution and a particular solution.

\[
\boxed{u(t)=u_c(t)+u_p(t)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.7)}
\]

The complementary part satisfies the homogeneous equation, and the particular part accounts for the harmonic force.

\textbf{B.6.8. Complementary solution.} For the underdamped case $0\leq\zeta<1$, define the damped frequency as

\[
\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}.
\]

The homogeneous equation then has the real solution

\[
\boxed{u_c(t)=Ce^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\sin(\omega_dt+\phi)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.8)}
\]

where $C$ and $\phi$ are determined by the initial conditions after the particular solution is included. Equivalently, the same response can be written as

\[
u_c(t)=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\left[A\cos(\omega_dt)+B\sin(\omega_dt)\right].
\]

\textbf{B.6.9. Particular solution.} Because the excitation is proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$, seek a particular solution in the same form,

\[
u_p(t)=\hat{u}_p e^{i\omega t}.
\]

Differentiation gives $\dot{u}_p=i\omega\hat{u}_p e^{i\omega t}$ and $\ddot{u}_p=-\omega^2\hat{u}_p e^{i\omega t}$. Substitution into the normalized ODE gives

\[
\left(-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega+\omega_n^2\right)\hat{u}_p=\hat{f}.
\]

Solving for the complex displacement amplitude gives

\[
\boxed{
\hat{u}_p=\frac{\hat{f}}
{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega},
\qquad
u_p(t)=\frac{\hat{f}e^{i\omega t}}
{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega}
}
\qquad\text{(B.6.9)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.10. Complete response and its meaning.} Combining the complementary and particular parts gives

\[
\boxed{
u(t)=Ce^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\sin(\omega_dt+\phi)
+\frac{\hat{f}e^{i\omega t}}
{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega}
}
\qquad\text{(B.6.10)}
\]

The first term is the transient response. It decays because of the factor $e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}$. The second term is the steady-state response. It remains after the transient has decayed and oscillates at the forcing frequency $\omega$. Initial conditions must be applied to the complete response, since both terms contribute at the start of the motion.

\textbf{B.6.11. Steady-state response amplitude.} The complex amplitude is the coefficient multiplying $e^{i\omega t}$ in the particular response.

\[
\boxed{
\hat{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\omega)=
\frac{\hat{f}}
{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega}
}
\qquad\text{(B.6.11)}
\]

Using $\hat{f}=\hat{F}/m$ gives the equivalent physical-parameter form

\[
\hat{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\omega)=
\frac{\hat{F}}{k-m\omega^2+ic\omega}.
\]

\textbf{B.6.12. Frequency response function.} The quasi-static displacement is obtained by taking $\omega\to0$.

\[
u_{\mathrm{qst}}=\frac{\hat{F}}{k}=\frac{\hat{f}}{\omega_n^2}.
\]

Normalize the steady-state amplitude by this displacement to obtain

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{FRF}(\omega)=\frac{\hat{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}}{u_{\mathrm{qst}}}
=\frac{\omega_n^2}
{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega}
}
\qquad\text{(B.6.12)}
\]

With $p=\omega/\omega_n$, this becomes

\[
\operatorname{FRF}(p)=\frac{1}{1-p^2+2i\zeta p}.
\]

\textbf{B.6.13. FRF variation with frequency.} At zero excitation frequency, $\operatorname{FRF}(0)=1$, so the response equals the quasi-static displacement and has zero phase lag. As $p$ increases, the magnitude can rise near the natural-frequency range and then decreases toward zero. The phase is negative because the displacement lags the applied force. It changes from $0$ at low frequency toward $-\pi$ as $p$ becomes large. Increasing $\zeta$ lowers and broadens the magnitude peak. When $\zeta\geq1/\sqrt{2}$, the magnitude has no nonzero interior peak.

\textbf{B.6.14. Magnification-factor definition.} The magnification factor is the magnitude of the normalized steady-state response.

\[
\boxed{M(\omega)=\left|\operatorname{FRF}(\omega)\right|
=\frac{|\hat{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}|}{u_{\mathrm{qst}}}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.14)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.15. Magnification factor in terms of $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$.} Taking the magnitude of the normalized FRF gives

\[
\boxed{
M(\omega)=
\frac{\omega_n^2}
{\sqrt{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+(2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}}
=\frac{1}
{\sqrt{(1-p^2)^2+(2\zeta p)^2}}
}
\qquad\text{(B.6.15)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.16. Resonance-frequency definition.} The true resonance frequency is the frequency at which the magnification factor is maximum.

\[
\boxed{\omega_r=\underset{\omega\geq0}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\ M(\omega)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.16)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.17. Resonance frequency expression.} It is convenient to minimize the reciprocal squared magnitude,

\[
M^{-2}(p)=Q(p)=(1-p^2)^2+4\zeta^2p^2.
\]

Differentiation gives

\[
\frac{dQ}{dp}=4p\left(p^2+2\zeta^2-1\right).
\]

The nonzero stationary point exists when $0\leq\zeta<1/\sqrt{2}$, which gives

\[
\boxed{\omega_r=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2},
\qquad 0\leq\zeta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.17)}
\]

For $\zeta\geq1/\sqrt{2}$, the square root is not real and the maximum occurs at the zero-frequency limit rather than at a nonzero resonance frequency.

\textbf{B.6.18. Peak magnification.} Substitute $p_r^2=1-2\zeta^2$ into the magnification expression. The result is

\[
\boxed{M_r=\frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}},
\qquad 0<\zeta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.18)}
\]

For zero damping, the ideal model has an unbounded response at $\omega=\omega_n$.

\textbf{B.6.19. FRF phase.} The phase is the argument of the complex FRF. Using the quadrant-aware inverse tangent gives

\[
\boxed{\phi(p)=\arg\!\left[\operatorname{FRF}(p)\right]
=-\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(2\zeta p,1-p^2\right)}
\qquad\text{(B.6.19)}
\]

This expression gives $\phi(0)=0$ and approaches $-\pi$ as $p\to\infty$.

\textbf{B.6.20. Phase resonance.} Phase resonance is the condition that the displacement lags the applied force by $90^\circ$, or $\phi=-\pi/2$. The real part of the FRF denominator must then vanish,

\[
1-p^2=0.
\]

For positive frequency, this gives $p=1$ and $\omega=\omega_n$. At this frequency,

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{FRF}(\omega_n)=\frac{1}{2i\zeta}=-\frac{i}{2\zeta}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.20)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.21. Magnification at phase resonance.} Taking the magnitude of the phase-resonance FRF gives

\[
\boxed{M_{90}=\left|\operatorname{FRF}(\omega_n)\right|=\frac{1}{2\zeta}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.21)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.22. Physical response at phase resonance.} Multiplying the normalized FRF by the quasi-static displacement gives the complex response amplitude,

\[
\hat{u}_{90}=u_{\mathrm{qst}}\operatorname{FRF}(\omega_n)
=-i\frac{\hat{F}}{2\zeta k}.
\]

Therefore, the physical response magnitude is

\[
\boxed{\left|\hat{u}_{90}\right|=\frac{\hat{F}}{2\zeta k}
=\frac{\hat{F}}{c\omega_n}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.22)}
\]

\textbf{B.6.23. True resonance versus phase resonance.} True resonance identifies the frequency at which the response magnitude is maximum. For light damping, it occurs at $\omega_r=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$, which is below $\omega_n$. Phase resonance identifies the frequency at which the response lags by $90^\circ$, and it occurs at $\omega=\omega_n$ for every positive damping ratio. These frequencies are equal only in the zero-damping limit. For $\zeta\geq1/\sqrt{2}$, there is no nonzero true-resonance peak, but the phase-resonance condition still occurs at $\omega_n$.

\textbf{B.6.24. Resonance and damped frequencies.} For an underdamped system,

\[
\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}.
\]

When $0<\zeta<1/\sqrt{2}$, the three frequencies satisfy

\[
\boxed{\omega_r=\omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}
<\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}<\omega_n}
\qquad\text{(B.6.24)}
\]

Thus, the true resonance frequency is below the damped free-vibration frequency, which is below the undamped natural frequency.

\textbf{B.6.25. Conversion from rad/s to RPM.} One complete excitation cycle per revolution corresponds to an angular speed of $2\pi$ radians per revolution. Therefore, a frequency $\omega$ in rad/s corresponds to

\[
\boxed{N_{\mathrm{RPM}}=\frac{60\omega}{2\pi}=\frac{30\omega}{\pi}\ \mathrm{RPM}}
\qquad\text{(B.6.25)}
\]
