Taking magnitudes in \(z^4=1\) gives \(\lvert z\rvert=1\), so write \(z=e^{i\theta}\). The equation then becomes

\[
e^{i4\theta}=1=e^{i2\pi k},\qquad k\in\mathrm{Z}.
\]

Therefore, \(4\theta=2\pi k\), or \(\theta=k\pi/2\). Four consecutive values of \(k\) give all distinct roots because increasing \(k\) by four adds one full revolution.

\[
\boxed{z\in\{1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i\}}
\qquad\text{(C.9.1.a)}
\]

The same roots written as complex exponentials are

\[
\boxed{z_k=e^{ik\pi/2},\qquad k=0,1,2,3}
\qquad\text{(C.9.1.b)}
\]

Euler's identity follows by combining the real and imaginary parts of a unit-magnitude complex exponential. Substituting \(\alpha=\omega t\) gives

\[
\boxed{e^{i\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t)}
\qquad\text{(C.9.2)}
\]

For a sinusoid written as \(x(t)=\sin(\omega t+\phi)\), the phase is \(\phi\). Define the phase difference as the phase of the second signal minus the phase of the first,

\[
\Delta\phi=\phi_2-\phi_1.
\]

A positive value means that \(x_2(t)\) leads \(x_1(t)\). In part (a), \(\phi_1=0\) and \(\phi_2=3\pi/4\), so

\[
\boxed{\Delta\phi_a=\frac{3\pi}{4}=135^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.3.a)}
\]

In part (b), subtract the first phase from the second phase and use a common denominator,

\[
\Delta\phi_b=\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{3}
=\frac{9\pi-4\pi}{12}=\frac{5\pi}{12}.
\]

Thus,

\[
\boxed{\Delta\phi_b=\frac{5\pi}{12}=75^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.3.b)}
\]

In part (c), the phases are already given in degrees. Their difference is

\[
\Delta\phi_c=115^\circ-45^\circ=70^\circ
=70\left(\frac{\pi}{180}\right)=\frac{7\pi}{18}.
\]

\[
\boxed{\Delta\phi_c=\frac{7\pi}{18}=70^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.3.c)}
\]

The phase of \(e^{i\theta}\) is \(\theta\) modulo \(2\pi\). Use the principal phase interval \((-\pi,\pi]\) when an angle has more than one equivalent representation. The requested phases are

\[
\boxed{\arg\left(e^{i\pi/2}\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}=90^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.4.a)}
\]

\[
\boxed{\arg\left(e^{i3\pi/2}\right)=\frac{3\pi}{2}=270^\circ
\equiv-\frac{\pi}{2}=-90^\circ\ \text{(principal)}}
\qquad\text{(C.9.4.b)}
\]

\[
\boxed{\arg\left(e^{i\pi/4}\right)=\frac{\pi}{4}=45^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.4.c)}
\]

\[
\boxed{\arg\left(e^{-i\pi/2}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{2}=-90^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.4.d)}
\]

\[
\boxed{\arg\left(e^{-i\pi/4}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{4}=-45^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.4.e)}
\]

For a unit-circle point, the real and imaginary coordinates are \(\bigl(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\bigr)\). Consequently, \(e^{i\pi/2}\) is at \((0,1)\), \(e^{i3\pi/2}\) is at \((0,-1)\), \(e^{i\pi/4}\) is at \((\sqrt{2}/2,\sqrt{2}/2)\), and \(e^{-i\pi/4}\) is at \((\sqrt{2}/2,-\sqrt{2}/2)\). The two angles \(3\pi/2\) and \(-\pi/2\) identify the same point, so \(e^{i3\pi/2}=e^{-i\pi/2}=-i\).

For \(Z=4+5i\), the real part is \(4\) and the imaginary part is \(5\). Its magnitude follows from the right triangle formed by those two components,

\[
\lvert Z\rvert=\sqrt{4^2+5^2}=\sqrt{41}=6.4031.
\]

Because \(Z\) lies in the first quadrant, its phase is the ordinary inverse tangent of the imaginary part divided by the real part,

\[
\arg Z=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)=0.8961\ \mathrm{rad}
=51.3402^\circ.
\]

\[
\boxed{\lvert Z\rvert=\sqrt{41}=6.4031,\qquad
\arg Z=0.8961\ \mathrm{rad}=51.3402^\circ}
\qquad\text{(C.9.6)}
\]
