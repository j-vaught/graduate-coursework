\textbf{A.3.1. Input data.} I measure the body coordinate $u(t)$ and road coordinate $u_b(t)$ upward from static equilibrium. The spring and damper act through their relative deformation, so the wheel displacement is the excitation input. The supplied diagram therefore represents base displacement excitation rather than an applied force.

For the equations of motion, the spring and damper forces depend on $u-u_b$ and $\dot u-\dot u_b$. Newton's law consequently gives
\[
m\ddot u+c(\dot u-\dot u_b)+k(u-u_b)=0,
\qquad
m\ddot u+c\dot u+ku=c\dot u_b+ku_b.
\]
With $\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $c/m=2\zeta\omega_n$, harmonic inputs $u_b=\Re\{\hat U_b e^{i\omega t}\}$ give the absolute body-to-road displacement ratio
\[
H(r)=\frac{\hat U}{\hat U_b}
=\frac{1+i2\zeta r}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r},
\qquad r=\frac{f}{f_n}.
\]
Its magnitude is
\[
|H(r)|=\sqrt{\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}}.
\]
The numerator follows from the base-motion terms on the right side of the equation of motion. It is also the absolute displacement transmissibility derived in the course notes (pp. 56--59).

\textbf{A.3.2. Wavy-road wavenumber.} One spatial period advances the phase by $2\pi$, so
\[
\gamma=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}
=\frac{2\pi}{32.004\ \mathrm{m}}
=0.196325\ \mathrm{rad/m}.
\]
\[\boxed{\gamma=0.1963\ \mathrm{rad/m}}\qquad\text{(A.3.2)}\]

\textbf{A.3.4. Road-frequency range.} At constant speed, $x=vt$ makes the road input $u_b(t)=a\cos(2\pi vt/\lambda)$, so $f=v/\lambda$. The maximum road frequency is therefore
\[
f_{\max}=\frac{v_{\max}}{\lambda}
=\frac{55.88\ \mathrm{m/s}}{32.004\ \mathrm{m}}
=1.74603\ \mathrm{Hz}.
\]
I evaluate $f=\operatorname{linspace}(0,1.74603\ \mathrm{Hz},2000)$ for the plotted curves.
\[\boxed{f_{\max}=1.746\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad N_f=2000}\qquad\text{(A.3.4)}\]

\textbf{A.3.6. Matched-frequency response.} At $r=1$, the complex response is $H(1)=1-i/(2\zeta)$, and its magnitude is
\[
|H_n|=\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{(2\zeta)^2}}
=\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{(0.440)^2}}=2.48300.
\]
The plotted analytic marker agrees with this value; linear interpolation on the 2000-point frequency curve gives the same value to the reported precision. For comparison, the true healthy maximum is $2.52688$ at $f=0.910396\ \mathrm{Hz}$, below $f_n$.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}
|H_n|&=2.48\quad\text{at }f=f_n=0.950\ \mathrm{Hz},\\
|H|_{\max}&=2.53\quad\text{at }f=0.910\ \mathrm{Hz}.
\end{aligned}}\qquad\text{(A.3.6)}\]

\textbf{A.3.7. Matched-frequency speed.} Since $f=v/\lambda$, setting $f=f_n$ gives
\[
v_n=f_n\lambda=(0.950\ \mathrm{Hz})(32.004\ \mathrm{m})
=30.4038\ \mathrm{m/s}=68.0114\ \mathrm{mph}.
\]
\[\boxed{v_n=30.4\ \mathrm{m/s}=68.0\ \mathrm{mph}}\qquad\text{(A.3.7)}\]

\textbf{(a) Matched-frequency event.} The speed conversion places $f_n$ at $v_n=68.0114\ \mathrm{mph}$, and the ordinate is $|H_n|=2.48300$. These agree with A.3.6 and A.3.7.

\textbf{(b) True peak event.} Differentiating $|H|^2$ with respect to $q=r^2$ gives the stationary condition
\[
4\zeta^2q^2+2q-2=0.
\]
The positive root $q=[-1+\sqrt{1+8\zeta^2}]/(4\zeta^2)$ for $\zeta=0.220$ gives $r_{\mathrm{cr}}=\sqrt{q}=0.958311$, $f_{\mathrm{cr}}=0.910396\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $v_{\mathrm{cr}}=29.1363\ \mathrm{m/s}=65.176\ \mathrm{mph}$, and $|H_{\max}|=2.52688$. This peak precedes the matched-frequency point.

\textbf{(c) Threshold crossings.} Setting $q=r^2$ and $L=1.5$ reduces $|H|=L$ to
\[
L^2q^2+\bigl[L^2(4\zeta^2-2)-4\zeta^2\bigr]q+(L^2-1)=0.
\]
There are two real positive crossings in the speed range for each shock condition. The first crossing is the limit encountered when accelerating from rest; the second is the speed above which amplification falls below 1.5 again.
\[
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\zeta=0.220:&\quad v_{\mathrm{low}}=18.33\ \mathrm{m/s}=41.0\ \mathrm{mph},\\
&\quad v_{\mathrm{high}}=37.6\ \mathrm{m/s}=84.1\ \mathrm{mph},\\
\zeta=0.100:&\quad v_{\mathrm{low}}=17.70\ \mathrm{m/s}=39.6\ \mathrm{mph},\\
&\quad v_{\mathrm{high}}=38.9\ \mathrm{m/s}=87.1\ \mathrm{mph}.
\end{aligned}}\qquad\text{(A.3.8)}\]

\textbf{A.3.9. Wavelength for a 35 mph matched speed.} To make the road frequency equal $f_n$ at $v_n=35\ \mathrm{mph}=15.6464\ \mathrm{m/s}$, choose
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{v_n}{f_n}
=\frac{15.6464\ \mathrm{m/s}}{0.950\ \mathrm{Hz}}
=16.4699\ \mathrm{m}.
\]
\[\boxed{\lambda_{\mathrm{new}}=16.47\ \mathrm{m}=54.0\ \mathrm{ft}}\qquad\text{(A.3.9)}\]

\textbf{A.3.10. Worn shocks.} Repeating items (5)--(8) with $\zeta=0.100$ retains the same matched frequency $f_n=0.950\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and matched speed $v_n=68.0114\ \mathrm{mph}$, but increases the matched response to
\[
|H_n|=\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{(0.200)^2}}=5.09902.
\]
The true peak is $|H|_{\max}=5.12277$ at $f_{\mathrm{cr}}=0.940818\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $v_{\mathrm{cr}}=30.1099\ \mathrm{m/s}=67.3540\ \mathrm{mph}$. The table compares both shock conditions. Since $|H|^2$ increases with damping ratio for $r>\sqrt{2}$, the healthy response exceeds the worn response at high frequency, even though lower damping produces a much larger resonance peak.
