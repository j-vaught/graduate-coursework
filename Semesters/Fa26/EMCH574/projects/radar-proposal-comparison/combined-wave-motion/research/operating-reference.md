# Reference radar and primary literature

## Documented reference and prescribed study variables

Furuno's DRS4D-NXT manual, specification sheet SP-1, reports a 25° vertical beamwidth at −3 dB and antenna speeds of 24, 36, and 48 rpm coupled to the range, or a selectable fixed 24 rpm. It gives a 3.9° typical horizontal beamwidth at −3 dB, bearing accuracy of ±1°, range resolution of 20 m, a minimum range of 20 m, and nominal 25 W output power. The reference case selects the supported fixed 24 rpm, corresponding to a 2.5 s revolution. The other two speeds correspond to 1.667 and 1.25 s, with the manual's range-coupling qualification. The beamwidth is a full width. Its one-way half-power angular offset is 12.5°; when the same one-way antenna pattern applies on transmit and receive, its total gain loss at that angle is 6.0206 dB. The full Gaussian shape is a study assumption, not a manufacturer pattern measurement.

Source: Furuno, *DRS4D-NXT radar sensor manual*, specification SP-1. The linked manual is in French, with speed units and numerical specifications given on sheet SP-1. https://www.furunousa.com/-/media/sites/furuno/document_library/documents/manuals/public_manuals/ifr36490a_drs4dnxt.pdf

The 0.50 m localization tolerance is prescribed for isolating motion-induced mapping error. It is not a demonstrated total localization accuracy for this radar. The manufacturer's 20 m range resolution and ±1° bearing accuracy motivate explicitly describing the model's exact noiseless range and bearing as idealized. Resolution is distinct from individual-target ranging precision, so the 20 m specification alone cannot quantify single-target random range error. The ±1° quoted accuracy corresponds to approximately 1.75 m cross-range at 100 m if interpreted as an uncompensated angular error. The experiment does not specify a manufacturer-calibrated detector margin, target radar cross section, multipath, clutter, or receiver noise figure. Threshold, buoy geometry, waves, vessel model, and response parameters therefore remain explicit prescribed study inputs.

## Related primary studies

Hessner et al. (2019), pp. 3–4, describe vessel-motion correction in shipborne marine radar wave/current measurements. In their space–time approach, GPS and heading estimate orientation and position for each radar pulse before polar-to-Cartesian georeferencing. They assume pitch, roll, and heave have no critical effect on WaMoS II, citing typical marine-radar vertical beamwidths of 20–25°. Their Polarstern system used a 2.5 s radar rotation. Their results concern sea-surface current retrieval on a research vessel and do not directly measure buoy detection or establish an angular loss limit for the selected small-vessel configuration. DOI https://doi.org/10.3390/rs11091030; author-institution PDF https://epic.awi.de/id/eprint/49633/1/remotesensing-11-01030-v2.pdf

Mar et al. (2013), Sections 1–2, simulate ship-motion beam pointing correction using a planar phased-array controller and estimate roll/pitch-induced pointing errors for tracking. This already establishes the value of correcting beam pointing and compares angular compensation for a different radar architecture and tracking objective. Our paired simulator's contribution, if supported by results, is to distinguish per-spoke mapping from idealized pointing recovery and report their impacts on scheduled buoy visits under wave-driven pitch and obstruction; it should not claim to introduce ship-motion beam compensation. https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/384756

Chen et al. (2014) study registration bias and observability for stabilized versus moving 3-D radar platforms with time-varying attitude and a Kalman filter. This establishes that attitude affects registration and that stabilized and unstabilized mounting have been compared in another formulation. Their outputs address systematic bias estimation and do not resolve our visibility-gated buoy-visit metric. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0373463313000799

Mounet et al. (2024), Sections 2 and 4–5 and Figures 13–14, estimate heave, pitch, and roll response models for an AutoNaut uncrewed vessel using operational inertial measurements and inferred sea state. Their model uses encounter-frequency-dependent hydrodynamic forcing, damping, and added mass for an assumed hull and compares response spectra with measurements. This provides relevant support for using wave-forced dynamics and illustrates why a generic fixed-coefficient resonator is a parametric response model rather than a validated AutoNaut response. Their plots are irregular-sea response spectra, not a supplied directly comparable sinusoidal response curve for the proposed model. Do not claim quantitative validation from this paper. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.measurement.2024.114724; accepted publisher PDF https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/368290749/1-s2.0-S0263224124006092-main.pdf

Jang et al. (2024) develop maritime radar scan features and semi-direct radar odometry using vessel experiments. They address motion estimation with marine radar imagery, which complements a study that assumes the pose is known. The paper does not validate echo probability or the vertical antenna model here. https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02773

These papers establish existing motion registration, attitude compensation and beam stabilization. The bounded question retained here is the size and mechanism of the paired improvement under declared model assumptions. This source set does not establish worldwide novelty.

Simpson et al. (2020), Section 4, report shipboard X-band radar data from vessels under 15 m with wave buoys in the imaging domain. They used image-to-image wave-field and buoy signatures to stabilize heading and georeference scans before wave forecasting. Their 44 rpm radar had a 1.25 s rotation and a 2.74 m antenna. They report that variable antenna height under ship motion may contribute to underestimated radar-derived wave slopes. This is a directly relevant small-vessel field context for wave shadowing and motion; their buoy images and analysis do not establish the usable-visit fraction or isolate the three interventions modeled here. https://doi.org/10.1175/JTECH-D-19-0127.1
