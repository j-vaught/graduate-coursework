\section*{Abstract}

The ultimate goal of this work is to predict whether the next radar scan will detect a small maritime target (within an acceptable position error) and identify the physical mechanism of target occlusion when detection fails.

\section{Introduction \& Objective}

In oceans, lakes, and other bodies of water (henceforth referred to as maritime environments), small targets can exhibit intermittent detection in radar returns. While the causes for this fading effect are numerous, the largest contributors, in no particular order, are wave geometry, radar beam motion induced by vessel pitch, and distorted scan positioning.

Clearly, it is desirable to eliminate these effects and, barring that, predict when they will occur. Here, we take advantage of the fact that the same forcing function—wavelike motion of the water surface—controls all three mechanisms. For simplicity, we assume that wind effects are not modeled independently and are represented through the wave field.

A wealth of literature already prescribes the individual mechanisms to build our model and the associated predictions. Waves and their interactions with vessels and objects are well-studied. In a similar fashion, inter- and intra-wave interactions (such as diffraction and propagation) are well-understood. The study of heave, pitch, and roll of vessels and their effects on radar scans is also well documented. Finally, the effects of radar beam distortion and general distortion against waves are also documented.

Therefore, the objective of our work is to combine these mechanisms into a single interpretable model for estimating the probability of small target detection in the subsequent radar scan. The model will primarily be tested in a synthetic environment, but an evaluation using real-world data will be included to validate the model's predictions, relying on publicly available datasets.


\section{Background \& Related Works}

Small maritime targets are nearly impossible to detect when their returns are comparable or weaker than surrounding sea clutter. As a solution, signal processing techniques, such as signal averaging, time-frequency analysis, and morphological filtering on real marine radar data have been utilized.

The work done by Panagopoulos and Soraghan evaluates these techniques and show that detection should be treated as an intermittent event since a target can become hidden or shadowed by waves on an interval. \cite{Panagopoulos2004SmallTarget} Their work, however, makes no attempt to predict these interruptions to detections in maritime environments.

This indicates, somewhat obviously, that wave geometry changes the radar path to a target and the illumination of the sea surface.
Plant and Farquharson develop some work on distinguishing geometric shadowing from partial shadowing, in which diffraction and weaker scattering can affect received signals. \cite{Plant2012WaveShadowing}
Their measurements show that a simple geometric blockage model does NOT explain all low-grazing-angle radar behavior.

Janssen et al. further show that diffraction changes the illumination field near and beyond wave-shadow boundaries, where geometric optics can misrepresent the illumination field. Of course, their work only considers the illumination field, and not the resulting radar return, and the latter half of the detection stage must be implemented in our work.
These findings motivate a geometric visibility baseline followed by a diffraction sensitivity model. \cite{Janssen2026Diffraction}

Because a rotating radar acquires azimuths (or spokes; vernacular can vary between literature) at different times, its scan is formed from measurements taken at different sensor positions and orientations. Work on shipborne radar georeferencing has already studied the importance of time-tagged measurements, position, heading, and installation offsets when mapping radar imagery to geographic coordinates. \cite{Lund2018SeaIceDrift}

Additional research on spinning radar shows that platform motion during acquisition can heavily distort a scan, requiring correction methods to be developed/utilized. Together, these studies motivate modeling the radar pose at each acquisition time and evaluating per-azimuth correction separately from losses that prevent a return from being detected. \cite{Burnett2021MotionDistortion} The full related-work discussion appears in Appendix~\ref{app:prior}.


\section{Method, Software, \& Data}


The primary experiment will use a MATLAB simulator to generate a shared wave field and propagate its effects through radar-vessel motion, target motion, illumination, and scan geometry. If MATLAB proves to be computationally intensive, alternative implementations in C++ may be considered. The field consists of prescribed linear wave components with defined amplitudes, frequencies, directions, and phases, each according to literature data.

Each component will be evaluated at the radar vessel and target locations, preserving their spatial phase relationship. Vessel heading and speed will determine the encounter frequency. Damped roll, pitch, and heave modes will represent vessel response, while a separate heave mode will represent target motion. The governing equations and response parameters are given in Appendix~\ref{app:forcing}.

The received echo will combine target scattering, antenna gain, two-way propagation, sea clutter, and receiver noise. Radar cross section (RCS) will represent the target scattering strength. A fixed detector, calibrated using development data, will determine whether the echo produces a detection. This separates losses that prevent a return from being acquired from errors introduced when an acquired return is mapped to position. The echo model and ray-time geometry are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:observation}.

The primary synthetic study will use known target geometry and complete wave, vessel, and target states, allowing each failure mechanism to be isolated and compared under matched conditions. Pose correction will be evaluated separately using midpoint pose, per-azimuth horizontal-motion correction, and per-azimuth attitude correction. Supporting model calculations and measured radar evidence appear in Appendix~\ref{app:published}; the evaluation dataset remains to be selected.



\section{Evaluation \& Metrics}

Four paired truth conditions will represent a reference case, vessel motion only, wave-path modulation only, and coupled motion and propagation. Each condition will use the same target trajectory, random draws, detector settings, and observation schedule. Within each condition, a history predictor will be compared with motion-feature, propagation-feature, and coupled predictors. Complete wave realizations, response regimes, and acquired files will be held out before fitting so adjacent observations cannot appear in both development and test data.

The primary forecast will estimate the probability that the target produces a usable observation on its next scheduled scan, using only history and state estimates available at the forecast time. A secondary forecast will estimate the probability of three consecutive missed visits. The Brier score will be the primary measure of probabilistic accuracy, with reliability diagrams used to assess calibration. Detection rate, false-alarm rate, missed-run length, outage duration, conditional position-error distributions, and processing time will describe operational performance.

The common forecast label will use one declared position-correction method and tolerance. A paired comparison will separately evaluate midpoint pose, per-azimuth horizontal-motion correction, and per-azimuth attitude correction. This separation ensures that differences in forecast scores reflect differences in predicted usable observations, while the correction comparison measures how mapping choices affect position quality among acquired returns. Misses will remain failures in the position analysis rather than being omitted as if their position error were zero. Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark} explains the comparison logic, and Appendix~\ref{app:parameter-design} justifies the numerical design.

\section{Shared wave forcing and mechanical response}

\label{app:forcing}

Linear Airy theory supplies the shared wave field through small-amplitude superposition and finite-depth dispersion. \cite[Sec.~6.2, pp.~3--6]{MITOCW2005LinearWaves} With surface elevation $\eta$, component amplitude $a_j$, propagation vector $\mathbf{k}_j$, angular frequency $\omega_j$, phase $\phi_j$, water depth $h$, and gravity $g$, the field is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:1}
\eta(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_j a_j\cos(\mathbf{k}_j\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega_j t+\phi_j),\quad\omega_j^2=g\lVert\mathbf{k}_j\rVert\tanh(\lVert\mathbf{k}_j\rVert h).
\end{equation}

For $\lVert\mathbf{k}_j\rVert h\gg1$, deep water gives $\omega_j^2=g\lVert\mathbf{k}_j\rVert$. A translating vessel follows $\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_0+\mathbf{U}t$. \cite[pp.~1--2]{Sclavounos2002TranslatingCoordinates} Substitution into Equation~\ref{eq:1} then gives the signed encounter frequency $\omega_{e,j}=\omega_j-\mathbf{k}_j\cdot\mathbf{U}$. Head seas increase this frequency because $\mathbf{k}_j$ points along propagation. Speed changes forcing frequency, while fixed oscillator parameters retain the same natural frequency.

The floating-structure equations supply inertia, damping, and restoring terms for a decoupled, small-motion approximation. \cite[pp.~165--167]{MITOCW2009FloatingResponse} For roll or pitch mode $q$, $I_q$ includes added inertia, $c_q$ is damping, $k_q$ is restoring stiffness, and $M_q$ is excitation moment. Oscillator normalization gives \cite{MITOCW2011DampedOscillator}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:2}
I_q\ddot\theta_q+c_q\dot\theta_q+k_q\theta_q=M_q(t),\quad\omega_{n,q}=\sqrt{k_q/I_q},\quad\zeta_q=\frac{c_q}{2\sqrt{k_q I_q}}.
\end{equation}

Heave similarly uses $m\ddot z+c\dot z+kz=F_w(t)$, with added mass included in $m$. Local elevation/slope excitation is a project assumption; dispersion does not determine its coefficients. Appendix~\ref{app:parameter-design} supplies numerical choices and their sources.

For harmonic moment amplitude $M_0$ and $\Omega=|\omega_e|$, Equation~\ref{eq:2} gives the complex amplitude $\Theta_q=M_0/(k_q-I_q\Omega^2+\mathrm{i}c_q\Omega)$ by the floating-body harmonic-response construction. \cite[pp.~166--167]{MITOCW2009FloatingResponse} Normalization by static deflection $M_0/k_q$ gives $H(r)=1/(1-r^2+2\mathrm{i}\zeta r)$, with $r=\Omega/\omega_n$. Dimensionless magnification and forcing-relative phase lag follow as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:3}
\lvert H(r)\rvert=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}},\qquad\delta(r)=\operatorname{atan2}(2\zeta r,1-r^2).
\end{equation}

Thus angular amplitude is $(M_0/k_q)|H(r)|$; wave-to-motion response additionally requires the excitation coefficient.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} connects the shared wave forcing to vessel dynamics and target--surface kinematics. Acquisition geometry and propagation determine the target echo, which the fixed detector evaluates with clutter and noise. Observation usability combines detection with corrected position quality, so acquisition geometry also feeds the output directly.

\section{From ray-time geometry to a usable observation}

\label{app:observation}

Mechanical response determines the radar origin and rotation at acquisition time $t_i$. With vessel reference position $\mathbf{p}_i$, body rotation $\mathbf{R}_{b,i}$, lever arm $\boldsymbol{\ell}$, and radar extrinsic rotation $\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{br}}$, use $\mathbf{s}_i=\mathbf{p}_i+\mathbf{R}_{b,i}\boldsymbol{\ell}$ and $\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{R}_{b,i}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{br}}$. Slant range $\rho_i$ and azimuth $\alpha_i$ define a family indexed by unmeasured elevation $\epsilon$,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:4}
\mathbf{q}_i(\epsilon)=\mathbf{s}_i+\rho_i\mathbf{R}_i\begin{pmatrix}\cos\epsilon\cos\alpha_i\\\cos\epsilon\sin\alpha_i\\\sin\epsilon\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

A known synthetic target-height plane closes this inverse. Public two-dimensional returns retain an elevation interval or target-height assumption. A general angular displacement bound cannot substitute for the horizontal error of this geometry. With acquisition geometry established, Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} isolates wave obstruction for a level radar. Its surface follows the linear wave model in Appendix~\ref{app:forcing}, with component amplitudes from the ISSC (Bretschneider) spectrum. \cite{Orcina2026WaveSpectra} Here $H_s$ is significant wave height and $T_p$ is peak period. The vertical scale is exaggerated, and the beamwidth annotation is schematic.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{wave-surface.pdf}
\caption{Wave obstruction for $H_s=1\,\mathrm{m}$, $T_p=9\,\mathrm{s}$, and $h=50\,\mathrm{m}$. The intervening crest prevents direct geometric rays from reaching the object.}
\label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

Let $B_i$ be one-way antenna power gain at the target direction and $G_i$ the one-way propagation power factor. The illustrative Gaussian beam uses full half-power width $\beta$ and $B_i=\exp[-4\ln(2)(\epsilon_i/\beta)^2]$. Figure~\ref{fig:beam-misalignment} illustrates this gain reduction when the target direction lies beyond a half-power direction.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{beam-misalignment.pdf}
\caption{Vertical beam misalignment. The target's angular offset $\epsilon_i$ exceeds the half-power angle $\beta/2$, reducing its normalized one-way antenna power gain below $1/2$.}
\label{fig:beam-misalignment}
\end{figure}

For an obstructing edge of height $d_c$ above the direct ray, wavelength $\lambda$, and path distances $d_1,d_2$, the limiting diffraction parameter is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:5}
\nu=d_c\sqrt{\frac{2(d_1+d_2)}{\lambda d_1d_2}},\quad J(\nu)=6.9+20\log_{10}\!\left(\sqrt{(\nu-0.1)^2+1}+\nu-0.1\right).
\end{equation}

The illustration sets $J=0$ for $\nu\leq-0.78$ and $G=10^{-J/10}$. The maximum normalized intrusion along the path represents one dominant obstacle. This ITU-R approximation supplies a check and sensitivity arm; a reflective smooth sea crest requires richer propagation treatment. \cite{ITUR2025P526}

For baseline target power $P_{0,i}$ and reciprocal complex propagation $q_i$ with $\lvert q_i\rvert^2=G_i$, a monostatic return is $Y_i=\sqrt{P_{0,i}}B_iq_i^2 e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{T,i}}+C_i+N_i$. Thus target power scales as $P_{0,i}B_i^2G_i^2$. Target phase, clutter $C_i$, noise $N_i$, range loss, polarization, and integration are explicit terms. Incident illumination alone is not a detection. \cite{Janssen2026Diffraction}

For detector decision $D_i$, correction method $\mathcal{C}$, horizontal error $E_i(\mathcal{C})$, and tolerance $E_{\max}$, define usable observation $U_i(\mathcal{C})=D_i\mathbf{1}\{E_i(\mathcal{C})\leq E_{\max}\}$. The probability forecast uses one frozen $\mathcal{C}$ so all predictors share labels. Separate correction experiments compare $U_i$ across mapping methods. Missing returns remain misses; they do not become zero-error positions.


Figure~\ref{fig:scan-time-error} isolates the position error caused by assigning the scan-midpoint radar position to an earlier acquisition. Under constant heading, the radar displacement transfers directly to the reconstructed target position.

\section{Published-model calculations}

\label{app:published}

The benchmark has two independent literature baselines. Wijaya and van Groesen construct surface visibility from geometric obstruction. Their published harmonic case uses period $T=9$ s, depth $h=50$ m, and radar-height ratios $H_r/A=5$ and $10$. \cite{Wijaya2016Shadowing} The published chapter gives the obstruction indicator and its image average as Equations 3.4 and 3.7. \cite{Wijaya2017Reconstruction} With a surface point $\eta(r,t)$ and radar height $H_r$, the equivalent skyline test is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:6}
\begin{aligned}\chi(r,t)&=\mathbf{1}\!\left\{\frac{\eta(r,t)-H_r}{r}\geq\sup_{0<x<r}\frac{\eta(x,t)-H_r}{x}\right\},\\V(r)&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\chi(r,t_j).\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For $\eta=A\cos(kr-\omega t)$, exact finite-depth dispersion gives $\lambda=124.829$ m. The reproduction uses the dissertation's Figure~3.4 case and averages $M=1400$ uniform phases on a grid of $\lambda/400$. It reproduces the paper's numerical visibility construction rather than digitizing its graph. In Figure~\ref{fig:visibility}, visibility at $r/\lambda=10$ is $0.153$ for $H_r/A=5$ and $0.235$ for $H_r/A=10$. Doubling phase and radial resolution changes the curves by at most $0.0011$. This surface-visibility baseline characterizes propagation rather than target detection probability.

\section{Benchmark rationale and comparison design}
\label{app:benchmark}

The central question is whether a shared estimate of waves and vessel response predicts the next usable target observation better than detection history or either mechanism alone. A comparison of unrelated scenes cannot answer that question because a change in sea state also changes target motion, clutter, and acquisition geometry. Thus, the benchmark separates changes to the physical generator from changes to the information supplied to the predictor. The former identifies which mechanisms cause loss; the latter tests whether estimating those mechanisms improves a forecast of the same observations.

Four physical conditions will be generated from each underlying realization. The target trajectory, wave phases, sensor configuration, scan clock, and background random draws remain common. The reference condition freezes the radar at its nominal pose and sets path transmission to unity. Motion only enables vessel motion and its effects on pointing and mapping. Propagation only enables wave-path attenuation while keeping the radar pose nominal. The coupled condition enables both. Target motion remains common to all four conditions, so changing a physical arm does not quietly substitute a different target experiment. Table~\ref{tab:truth-worlds} states the interventions explicitly.

Pairing also makes the numerical comparison more efficient. If two score estimates are $S_a$ and $S_b$, then $\operatorname{Var}(S_a-S_b)=\operatorname{Var}(S_a)+\operatorname{Var}(S_b)-2\operatorname{Cov}(S_a,S_b)$. Common random numbers reduce comparison variance when they induce positive covariance; their benefit is therefore checked rather than assumed. \cite{Glasserman1992CommonRandomNumbers} The primary ablation will hold the background process common to isolate target-path and antenna effects. However, because real sea clutter can also change with waves and look direction, a separate sensitivity comparison will vary its conditional distribution. Otherwise, an apparent propagation benefit could simply reflect an unrealistically fixed clutter field.

Within each physical condition, four predictors will use the information sets in Table~\ref{tab:predictor-groups}. The history predictor establishes how much persistence alone explains. Motion features add estimated vessel state and acquisition geometry. Propagation features add estimated wave and target state. The coupled predictor adds both groups and a small set of declared phase, pointing, and transmission interactions. Each predictor will use logistic regression with the same regularization-selection and probability-calibration procedure. Greater input dimension is allowed, but a different model family or a larger tuning search is not introduced only for the coupled case. Ergo, the comparison tests added physical information under a common learning procedure.

The simulator will retain complete physical truth, but the predictors will receive only estimates available at the forecast cutoff. A perfect-state predictor can be reported as an oracle diagnostic, separately from the operational comparison. All state errors will be applied before generating predictor inputs; future target position, future waves, and test outcomes will not be exposed during fitting. Future states may be propagated from the cutoff estimates using frozen dynamics, while subsequent simulator truth remains reserved for evaluation. Thus, knowing the synthetic truth does not automatically give the forecaster the answer.

Let $D_i$ denote a correctly associated target detection, and let $E_i(\mathcal{C}_0)$ be its horizontal position error under one frozen correction method $\mathcal{C}_0$. With tolerance $E_{\max}$, the next-scan event follows the usable-observation definition in Appendix~\ref{app:observation}. The primary probability $p_i$ and the separate three-miss event $V_i$ are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forecast-events}
\begin{aligned}
p_i&=\Pr\!\left(U_{i+1}(\mathcal{C}_0)=1\mid\mathcal{F}_i\right),\\
V_i&=\prod_{j=1}^{3}(1-D_{i+j}),\qquad
q_i=\Pr(V_i=1\mid\mathcal{F}_i).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{F}_i$ contains only history and estimated state available at the cutoff. The next visit is defined by its azimuth acquisition time, rather than by the scan midpoint. The three-miss probability will be fitted as a joint sequence event; multiplying three marginal miss probabilities would impose independence precisely where waves create temporal dependence. A three-visit outage horizon is a proposed tracking stress case, while the next-scan horizon is the main research question.

The Brier score measures the quality of these probabilities before any warning threshold is chosen. Using $N$ eligible forecasts, its binary form is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:brier}
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[p_i-U_{i+1}(\mathcal{C}_0)\right]^2.
\end{equation}
The same calculation scores $q_i$ against $V_i$. Brier introduced probability-based forecast verification, and the proper-scoring framework explains why the expected score is minimized by the true event probability. \cite{Brier1950Verification,Gneiting2007ProperScoring} However, a favorable average can hide a predictor that mainly repeats the event prevalence. Therefore, reliability diagrams will show observed frequencies within forecast-probability groups, and a constant probability estimated on development data will accompany the history baseline. Results will be reported within each physical condition and held-out regime rather than averaged across conditions that contain different amounts of usable data.

Correction methods will be compared in a separate experiment using the same detected target associations and paired scenes. Midpoint pose, per-azimuth horizontal motion, and per-azimuth attitude treatment will each map the acquired returns, following the acquisition-time registration problem studied by Lund et al. and Burnett et al. \cite{Lund2018SeaIceDrift,Burnett2021MotionDistortion} The forecast labels remain based on $\mathcal{C}_0$. In contrast, the correction experiment will report how each mapping changes conditional error and usable fraction. Position error is undefined for a missed target, so misses will count against joint usability and be reported alongside conditional errors; no zero error will be assigned to an absent return.

Complete wave realizations and acquired files will be assigned to development, calibration, or test partitions before any fitting. Within the synthetic test, selected frequency and response regimes will also remain outside development. Roberts et al. show why random splitting of structurally dependent observations can underestimate prediction error. \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation} Here, adjacent scans share waves and target dynamics, so a random scan split would reward recognition of a previously seen trajectory. Probability calibration, regularization, feature scaling, and detector choices will therefore be frozen before opening the test partition. An experimental dataset has not yet been selected; this grouping rule will be applied to the independent acquisition unit supported by that dataset.

The primary comparison will use realization-level paired differences between the coupled predictor and the history, motion, and propagation predictors. Bootstrap resampling will draw complete independent realizations, keeping all scans and paired methods together. This applies Efron's resampling principle to the independent experimental unit rather than treating correlated scans as independent observations. \cite{Efron1979Bootstrap} Intervals will be computed within held-out regimes. Evidence for the proposed contribution requires consistent improvement over both single-mechanism predictors, with uncertainty excluding zero for the declared paired comparisons. A gain over history alone would show that physical features help, but would not establish that their coupling is necessary. Thus, the main inference comes from paired forecasts on common labels, rather than from a score contrast whose value depends on the chosen scale.

\subsection{Parameter design and numerical justification}
\label{app:parameter-design}

The benchmark above determines what must change, while parameter selection determines whether those changes are physically interpretable. Published models supply dispersion, response structure, and acquisition reference cases. The numerical ranges below are proposed study settings chosen to span those mechanisms. They will be filtered by the governing equations and convergence checks, rather than treated as independent boxes of plausible numbers. Literature-derived reference values and calculated constraints are identified separately in the tables.

\subsubsection{Wave field and admissible amplitude}

Linear wave theory expands about a small free-surface slope and relates frequency, depth, and wavelength through the dispersion relation. \cite{MITOCW2005LinearWaves} Thus, amplitude cannot be selected independently of frequency. With $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, the deep-water screening calculation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wave-screen}
k=\frac{(2\pi f)^2}{g},\qquad
\lambda=\frac{2\pi}{k},\qquad
ka\leq0.20\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a\leq\frac{0.20}{k}.
\end{equation}
The cutoff $0.20$ is the proposed linear-model eligibility screen, not a universal breaking threshold. Table~\ref{tab:wave-choices} retains the intended amplitude and frequency ranges, and Table~\ref{tab:wave-constraints} shows why some combinations must be removed. At finite depth, the full relation in Appendix~\ref{app:forcing} will be solved before screening. For a multicomponent surface, the realized combined slope and exposure will also be checked; satisfying the component screen alone does not ensure a linear combined field.

\subsubsection{Detector operating point and trial budget}

A detector must be common across methods, because changing its threshold would change the target event being forecast. The initial choice is cell-averaging constant false-alarm-rate (CA-CFAR) detection with a development-calibrated scaling factor. Rohling describes local reference-cell thresholding and compares its behavior in uniform clutter, clutter edges, and multiple-target situations. \cite{Rohling1983RadarCFAR} Accordingly, homogeneous exponential-power clutter will provide the analytical check, while compound-Gaussian backgrounds and clutter edges will test robustness. The guard region will be set using development-only target-spread checks before any forecast evaluation. The unattenuated target signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) will be varied while also recording its power margin above the clutter-dependent threshold.

For $N_r$ independent exponential reference powers and a mean-based threshold, the analytical relation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cfar-check}
P_{\mathrm{FA}}=(1+\alpha/N_r)^{-N_r},\qquad
\alpha=N_r\left(P_{\mathrm{FA}}^{-1/N_r}-1\right).
\end{equation}
At $N_r=24$ and $P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.01$, this gives $\alpha=5.077$. This relation checks the implementation under its stated distribution, while the empirical threshold calibration controls the proposed nonhomogeneous cases. The detector uses the same cells, association rule, and scaling procedure for all predictor groups.

\section{Related Works}
\label{app:prior}

The preceding benchmark combines established wave, propagation, detection, and mapping models. The relevant literature therefore needs to be read by the quantity each study predicts or measures. Surface visibility, received backscatter, a detector decision, and geographic position are different outcomes; the proposed forecast connects them through a common state and a declared usable-observation event.

\subsection{Small-target detection and sea clutter}

The sea-clutter literature makes the target decision conditional on the received field. Panagopoulos and Soraghan's three processing families suppress unwanted echo structure through averaging, time-frequency separation, and morphological filtering, and their real-data examples show that small-target extraction is feasible under clutter variability. \cite{Panagopoulos2004SmallTarget} However, these operations do not supply a physical explanation for a missed visit. A low detector response may result from clutter masking, target aspect, propagation loss, antenna gain, or a target return arriving between useful beam samples. The proposed model therefore carries the target echo, sea clutter, and receiver noise as separate terms before applying a fixed detection rule. This formulation preserves the distinction between an unobserved target and an observed target whose geographic position is inaccurate. It also makes the forecast label operational. A usable observation requires both a detector decision and a position error below the declared tolerance.

\subsection{Wave shadowing, partial illumination, and diffraction}

Wave geometry supplies an important propagation baseline, but the evidence does not support treating visibility as a binary law. Plant and Farquharson distinguish geometric shadowing, in which incident rays are completely blocked, from partial shadowing, in which diffraction and weak scattering can leave a reduced return in a geometrically shadowed region. Their coherent X-band measurements test how the fraction of near-noise samples changes with incident power and polarization. The observed behavior does not support geometric shadowing as a dominant explanation for low-grazing-angle deep-water microwave backscatter, while polarization-dependent partial-shadowing effects become more relevant for steep waves near shorelines. \cite{Plant2012WaveShadowing} The implication is direct. A geometric line-of-sight indicator is useful as a sensitivity baseline, yet it cannot be interpreted as target detection probability or as proof that a return is absent. The received target term must retain propagation attenuation, clutter, noise, polarization, and detector-threshold effects.

Wijaya and van Groesen use a synthetic radar-image construction to relate ocean-surface visibility to geometric obstruction. Their analysis gives a deterministic connection between normalized radar height, range, wave elevation, and the fraction of a surface record that remains visible. \cite{Wijaya2016Shadowing} The associated dissertation reproduces the obstruction indicator and image-average construction in its third chapter, including the harmonic parameter cases used to examine significant wave height and wave reconstruction. \cite{Wijaya2017Reconstruction} These works provide a useful geometry-first reference because a shared wave field can be evaluated at the radar and target locations with a common phase. However, surface visibility is an intermediate state. It does not include target radar cross section, antenna beam gain, two-way propagation, sea clutter, receiver noise, or the scan-time decision that determines whether a target is detected. The present study consequently uses geometric obstruction to expose a mechanism and then propagates the same state into an explicit received-echo model.

Diffraction sharpens the distinction between incident illumination and a usable target observation. Janssen et al. calculate diffraction effects in radar illumination of sea waves and show that the field near and beyond a wave-shadow boundary can differ from a geometric-optics construction. \cite{Janssen2026Diffraction} Their result supports a diffraction sensitivity arm around the geometric baseline. It does not by itself establish a target-return or detection model, because illumination is only one factor in the monostatic echo. Ergo, the relevant forecast state must include the target path, the two-way propagation factor, antenna response, and clutter and noise conditions under which the detector operates. This coupling also avoids attributing every missed observation to wave blockage when partial illumination or weak scattering provides a competing explanation.

\subsection{Wave-driven mechanical response}

The waves that change propagation also excite the radar vessel. Linear wave-body theory connects incident waves to body motion through hydrodynamic forcing, restoring forces, inertia, and damping. Its response-amplitude operators retain frequency-dependent gain and phase, while encounter frequency accounts for vessel speed and heading. \cite{MITOCW2002WaveBody} Consequently, speed changes the forcing experienced by the vessel even when its prescribed natural frequencies remain fixed. Wave phase controls both the obstructing surface and radar attitude; independently drawing those states would discard the relationship the project aims to test.

The initial damped modes preserve resonance and phase lag while keeping the semester study tractable. Their swept effective parameters are design coordinates. Once a vessel and acquisition are selected, measured response-amplitude operators can replace them. Thus, the benchmark tests shared forcing before vessel-specific identification, while retaining the same forecast event and paired comparisons.

\subsection{Scan-time motion and correction}

A rotating radar does not acquire a scan at one instant. Each azimuth sample is associated with its own acquisition time, sensor position, orientation, and beam direction. Lund et al. demonstrate pulse-by-pulse georeferencing for shipboard marine radar by combining time-tagged radar measurements with interpolated vessel position and heading. Their radar completes a rotation in $1.25\,\mathrm{s}$ and maps pulses on a $7.5\,\mathrm{m}$ Cartesian grid. Heave, pitch, and roll are omitted because their effects are judged small relative to that resolution and expected to average out. Thus, the work supplies a horizontal-motion baseline and a stated scale argument, rather than evidence that vertical motion can always be ignored. \cite{Lund2018SeaIceDrift} McCann and Bell address a related registration problem for ship-borne X-band intensity imagery. They identify finite angular, range, and timing offsets introduced by equipment installation and estimate those offsets by maximizing the sharpness of time-integrated imagery formed from radar images and high-frequency vessel navigation data. \cite{McCann2018OffsetCalibration} Together, these studies establish that installation and acquisition errors can degrade the location of an acquired return. The proposed correction comparison will use their time-aware mapping principle, while separately perturbing the vessel attitude and radar-to-navigation lever arm.

Spinning-radar navigation work makes the scan-distortion mechanism explicit. Burnett, Schoellig, and Barfoot show that motion distortion and Doppler effects can materially affect radar odometry, and they evaluate an estimator that accounts for both effects on spinning-radar scans. \cite{Burnett2021MotionDistortion} Their experiments concern automotive radar navigation, so the platform and scene differ from a wave-driven maritime vessel. The transferable result is the acquisition principle. Motion during a scan changes the geometry represented by the assembled image, and a midpoint-pose approximation needs to be tested against the time structure of the scan. For the present proposal, vessel heave, pitch, roll, translation, and sensor offsets are evaluated at each azimuth time. Correction quality is then assessed separately from the physical processes that prevent a target echo from crossing the detector threshold.

\subsection{Research gap and proposed connection}

These studies supply complementary pieces of a reliability model. Detection work addresses extraction from clutter; shadowing and diffraction work describes illumination; shipboard and spinning-radar studies address acquisition-time registration. However, their reconstruction, detection, and navigation objectives do not individually define the joint next-scan event. The benchmark tests whether connecting these states improves that forecast, with novelty assessed against the resulting evidence and final literature comparison.

Thus, the contribution is an interpretable forecast using a common wave realization to connect body response, target state, propagation, beam gain, and scan geometry. The predictor receives history and state estimates available before the next scan. The label records whether that scan yields a detected target whose corrected position satisfies the tolerance. This common event tests the predictive value of coupling while retaining separate explanations for propagation loss, clutter masking, beam loss, and position distortion.
